Data Broker Removal: How to Remove Yourself From 25 Sites (2026 Guide)

If you have ever searched your own name and found your address, age, phone number and relatives on a website you never signed up for, you have met a data broker. It is an unsettling moment, and you are far from alone. The good news is that almost every one of these sites lets you remove yourself for free. This guide walks you through it one calm step at a time.

By Siddharth Shankar Updated: November 10, 2026 Covers: 25 sites Jump to: the opt-out list
TL;DR: how data broker removal works

Search your name, phone number and email to find the sites that list you. Opt out of the biggest people-search sites first, using each site's own free form. Most forms take about ten minutes, and most sites say they finish within one to seven days. Some sites share one opt-out: a single PeopleConnect request covers TruthFinder, Instant Checkmate, Intelius and US Search. If you live in California, one free DROP request reaches more than 600 registered brokers. Keep a log and recheck every three months, because listings can come back. If you would rather not do it all by hand, compare data removal services by country.

What is a data broker?

A data broker is a company that collects information about people from many places, joins it into a profile and sells or shares it. You usually never deal with these companies directly, which is why finding yourself on one feels so strange.

Brokers come in three broad types, and it helps to know which one you are dealing with:

  • People-search sites. Sites such as Whitepages, Spokeo and TruePeopleSearch let anyone type in a name and see a profile. These are the ones most people stumble on, and they are the focus of this guide.
  • Marketing data brokers. They build lists of people by interests, life events or spending habits and sell them to advertisers. You rarely see their files, but they shape the junk mail and ads you receive.
  • Risk and identity brokers. Companies such as LexisNexis Risk Solutions supply data used for fraud checks, insurance and credit offers. Their opt-outs work differently, so they get their own row in the list below.

For the bigger picture, read what data brokers are and why they have your information.

Where data brokers get your information

Most broker profiles are stitched together from records that were each shared somewhere along the way. Common sources include:

  • property and land records, which often show your home address
  • voter registration files, where state law allows their use (see what your voter record reveals)
  • old phone books and directory listings
  • court and other public records
  • marketing lists built from apps, loyalty schemes, surveys and online sign-ups
  • other data brokers, which buy, sell and swap data with each other

That last source explains why removal can feel like a game of whack-a-mole. When one site copies from another, a listing you removed can be rebuilt from a source you never touched. That is not your fault, and it does not mean your opt-out failed. It simply means data broker removal works best as a routine rather than a one-off job.

Data broker removal in six steps

  1. Search for yourself.

    Search your full name with your current city, then with a past city, then your phone number and email address. Note every site that shows a profile that is really you. Common names bring up strangers, so check two details, such as a relative's name and a past address.

  2. Use a separate email address.

    Most opt-outs send a confirmation link. An email alias keeps those messages in one place and keeps your main inbox off yet another list.

  3. Start with the biggest sites.

    Remove yourself first from the sites that rank highest for your name, because those are the ones people actually see. The list below is in roughly that order.

  4. Follow each site's own form.

    Copy the exact listing URL when a form asks for it, give only the details the form needs and click the verification link. The FTC notes that opting out can require some information to verify your identity, so be wary of any site that asks for more than its form really needs.

  5. Log every request.

    Write down the site, the listing URL, the date and how you submitted it. If a listing comes back, this log saves you from starting over.

  6. Recheck every three months.

    Search for yourself again, confirm each removal and resubmit where needed. Our guide on how to verify a removal actually worked shows what to look for.

One opt-out can cover several sites

Some people-search brands belong to the same company, and that can save you a lot of time:

  • PeopleConnect. TruthFinder, Instant Checkmate, Intelius and US Search all use PeopleConnect's free Suppression Center. One suppression hides your report from name searches on all four. It does not cover phone, address or email searches, so check those separately.
  • LexisNexis. One consumer portal handles three different opt-outs: prescreened credit and insurance offers, direct marketing, and a Public Safety suppression for people who can show proof, such as a protective order.
  • Lookalike sites. FastPeopleSearch and TruePeopleSearch look similar, but each has its own removal page. Sites that share data can still require their own request.

US laws and data broker registries that help you

Four US states require data brokers to register: California, Vermont, Texas and Oregon. Registries are useful because they name companies you would never find by searching your name. They are not complete, though. When the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse compared the registries in 2025, it found hundreds of brokers registered in one state but not in another.

California: one request for every registered broker

California residents can use DROP, the Delete Request and Opt-out Platform run by the California Privacy Protection Agency. It opened on January 1, 2026, it is free, and one request reaches more than 600 registered data brokers. Since August 1, 2026, brokers must check DROP at least once every 45 days and process deletions. Our California DROP guide explains how to file and what DROP does not cover.

Vermont: a new deletion right from 2027

Vermont has kept a data broker registry since 2019. In June 2026 the state enacted Act 138, which gives consumers the right to ask data brokers to delete their brokered personal information from January 1, 2027. Brokers will generally have 30 days to act and must offer their own deletion page. There is no single portal yet; the state is studying one.

Texas and Oregon: registries you can search

Texas requires data brokers to register with the Secretary of State. Since September 1, 2025, each registration must link to instructions for using your rights under the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act. Oregon has run its own registry through the Division of Financial Regulation since 2024. Both registries let you look up which companies have registered.

Your rights under state privacy laws

If your state has a broad privacy law, you can usually ask a company to delete your data or stop selling it. See our guides for California, Colorado, Connecticut, Oregon, Texas and Virginia.

Data broker removal outside the US

People-search sites are mostly an American business, but data brokers trade information everywhere. Your tools depend on where you live:

  • United Kingdom. Under UK GDPR you can ask any organisation to delete your data, and it must normally reply within one calendar month. You can also object to direct marketing at any time. A good first step is to opt out of the open register, then send UK GDPR erasure requests.
  • European Union. The GDPR gives you a right to erasure. Ask each company directly using our GDPR erasure guide, and ask Google separately to delist results about you.
  • Canada. PIPEDA lets you see and correct your information and withdraw consent, but it has no explicit erasure right. Quebec adds a de-indexing remedy. See our PIPEDA guide.
  • Australia. The Privacy Act does not yet give a general right to have your data erased. Draft reforms released in August 2026 propose one. For now you can ask for corrections and cut marketing calls with the Do Not Call Register.

Paid removal services also vary by country: some work in the UK, Canada and Australia, and others only in the US. Our comparison has a country-by-country availability table.

How long does data broker removal take?

Each form usually takes about ten minutes. After that, processing ranges from a few minutes at FastPeopleSearch to five to seven days at CheckPeople, and LexisNexis allows up to 30 days. Even after a page is gone, Google can keep showing an old result for a while; our Whitepages guide notes that search engine copies can take up to 30 days to drop.

The first full pass is the slow part. After that, a quarterly recheck is much quicker, because you only redo the sites where you have reappeared.

Can data brokers put your information back?

Yes, and it is one of the most frustrating parts of the process. A listing can return when you move house, change your phone number or appear in a new public record. It can also come back when a site refreshes its data from another broker, or when a second profile is built under a different version of your name.

If that happens, check your log, resubmit the opt-out and note the date. Our guide on what to do when removed information keeps reappearing covers the common causes and fixes.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remove my information from data brokers?

Search for your name, phone and email to find the sites that list you. Then use each site's own opt-out form, starting with the biggest ones, confirm by email, log the request and recheck in three months. The table above links to step-by-step guides for 25 sites.

Can I remove my information from data brokers for free?

Yes. Every opt-out in our list is free, and California residents can file one free DROP request that reaches every registered broker. Paid services save time, but you never have to pay to remove yourself.

How much does a data removal service cost?

Most charge a yearly subscription, and prices vary by service, plan and country. Our comparison of data removal services lists current prices and money-back terms.

Will removing my information from one data broker remove it everywhere?

No. Each company keeps its own database, so each needs its own request. The exceptions are shared opt-outs such as PeopleConnect, where one request covers four sites.

Is it safe to give a data broker my email to opt out?

Most forms only need an email address to send a confirmation link. Using a separate email alias keeps your main inbox private. Be cautious with any site that asks for an ID upload when a simple form would do.

Can data brokers add my information again?

Yes. New records and data refreshes can create a fresh listing. That is why a three-monthly recheck matters, and why many people choose a service that rechecks for them.
