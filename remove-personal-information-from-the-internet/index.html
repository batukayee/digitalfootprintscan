How to Remove Personal Information From the Internet (2026 Guide)

Finding your home address, phone number or old photos online can feel like a door has been left open without your permission. You can close most of those doors yourself, for free, once you know which tool fits which problem. This guide shows you exactly that, one step at a time.

By Siddharth Shankar Updated: November 10, 2026 Jump to: where to start

Removing personal information from the internet is rarely one request. Each place your details appear has its own owner and its own removal route: a website owner, a data broker, Google, a social network. The trick is to match each piece of information to the right route, start with what strangers are most likely to see, and then keep it from coming back.

Quick answer: how to remove personal information from the internet

Search your name, emails, phone numbers and old usernames to see what is exposed. Opt out of people-search sites and data brokers first, because they publish your address and phone number to anyone. Then ask Google to drop results using Results about you, delete or lock down old accounts, and use your legal rights where you live. Almost all of this is free. Recheck every three months, because listings can return. If you would rather hand it over, compare data removal services by country.

Notice the pattern: Google can hide a result, but only the source can delete the page. That is why the most lasting fixes happen at the source. Our guide on removing information from Google versus removing it from the internet explains the difference in more detail.

Step 1: Find out what is exposed

You cannot remove what you have not found, so start with a short, honest search. Use a private browser window so your results are not shaped by your own history.

  1. Search your full name.

    Put your name in quotation marks, then add your city, a past city, your employer or a maiden name. Look at the first two pages of results.

  2. Search your email addresses.

    Include old ones. An old email can still be tied to profiles, forum posts and accounts you forgot about.

  3. Search your phone number.

    Try it with and without dashes and spaces. Phone numbers often lead straight to a people-search profile with your address.

  4. Search your old usernames.

    Reused usernames link accounts together. See what people can find from your username.

  5. Write it all down.

    Note each page, what it shows and who runs it. This list becomes your removal plan.

Step 2: Remove yourself from people-search sites

Start here, because people-search sites are what most strangers find first. They show your current address, phone number, age and relatives to anyone who types your name. Almost every one offers a free opt-out. Begin with the biggest:

  • Whitepages: paste your listing URL and verify with a phone code.
  • Spokeo: paste your profile URL and confirm by email.
  • TruePeopleSearch and FastPeopleSearch: each has its own removal page.
  • TruthFinder: one PeopleConnect request also covers Instant Checkmate, Intelius and US Search.
  • BeenVerified: search by name and state, then confirm by email.

Then work through the rest using our opt-out list for 25 sites and the complete opt-out routine.

Step 3: Remove your data from data brokers

Behind the people-search sites sit hundreds of data brokers that trade information you never see. You cannot search most of them by name, so the law does some of the work:

  • California: one free DROP request reaches more than 600 registered data brokers.
  • Other US states with privacy laws: you can ask companies to delete your data or stop selling it.
  • UK and EU: you can send an erasure request to any company that holds your data.

Our data broker removal guide covers the registries, the laws by country and the brokers you can opt out of directly.

Step 4: Remove your information from Google

Most people find personal information through Google, so cleaning up results cuts most of the traffic to it. Google will not remove anything you would prefer to hide, but its policy does cover:

  • contact details such as your home address, phone number and email
  • government ID numbers, bank account and card numbers
  • images of your signature or ID documents
  • private records such as medical records, and confidential login details
  • doxxing: pages that share your details with threats or to harass you

Pick the tool that matches what you found:

  • Your address, phone or email: use Results about you. It searches for your details, lets you request removal in a few clicks and alerts you to new matches. In the US, Google has added ID numbers such as driver's license and Social Security numbers.
  • ID numbers, financial details, private records or doxxing: use Google's personal information removal form. Our guide to removing personal information from Google Search walks through it.
  • A page that has already changed or been deleted: use Google's Refresh outdated content tool so the old snippet disappears.
  • Photos of you: see how to remove photos of yourself from Google.

One honest warning: an approved request hides the result in Google, but the page itself stays online and can still show up elsewhere. That is why removed information can still appear in search results.

Step 5: Delete or lock down old accounts and social profiles

Old accounts are the part of a cleanup people forget most. A forum you joined in 2012 or a shopping account you used once can still show your name, photo or email.

  • Find forgotten accounts with our guide to finding old accounts you forgot about, then delete the ones you no longer need.
  • Set the profiles you keep to private, or trim what strangers can see: Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn and X.
  • Remove your home address and phone number from any public profile or bio.

Step 6: Hide your home from maps and property sites

Your house can be as identifying as your name. Two quick fixes help a lot: ask Google to blur your house on Street View, and hide or correct your home on Zillow and Redfin. It is also worth knowing how property records make your address public, because that is where many listings start.

Step 7: Use your legal rights where you live

Privacy laws give you more leverage than a polite request:

  • United States: state laws such as California's let you ask a company to delete your data. See how to send a CCPA deletion request.
  • United Kingdom: an organisation must normally reply to an erasure request within one calendar month. See our UK GDPR erasure guide.
  • European Union: the GDPR gives you a right to erasure, and you can ask Google to delist results under the right to be forgotten.
  • Canada: PIPEDA lets you access and correct your data and withdraw consent. See our PIPEDA guide.
  • Australia: there is no general right to erasure yet; draft reforms released in August 2026 propose one.

What you cannot remove, and what to do instead

Some things cannot be taken down, and it is kinder to know that early:

  • Data from breaches. Once your email and password leak, copies circulate. You cannot recall them, but you can make them useless: change reused passwords, turn on two-step sign-in and check whether your password has leaked.
  • Public records. Court and property records usually stay public. Read what can be sealed, and focus on removing the people-search copies.
  • News stories. Publishers rarely delete accurate reporting. In Europe you may be able to ask Google to delist old, irrelevant results.
  • Identity theft risk. If your Social Security number is exposed, a credit freeze is the strongest free protection.

How to remove personal information from the internet for free

Every core tool in this guide costs nothing:

  • people-search and data broker opt-out forms
  • California's DROP
  • Google's Results about you, removal form and Refresh outdated content
  • account deletion and privacy settings
  • legal erasure and deletion requests, which are usually free

What a paid service buys you is time: someone else does the searching, the forms and the rechecks.

Should you do it yourself or use a removal service?

Doing it yourself makes sense if only a handful of sites list you and you are happy to recheck every few months. It is free and gives you full control.

A data removal service makes sense when you appear on many sites, when you are short on time, or when your safety matters and you want listings watched continuously. Before you pay, check three things: whether it works in your country, how many sites it covers and whether it offers a money-back guarantee. How to keep your information from coming back

  • Use an email alias for sign-ups, so your main address does not spread.
  • Give a second phone number to shops and apps.
  • Delete accounts you no longer use.
  • Keep contact details off public profiles.
  • Search for yourself every three months, and resubmit any opt-out that has lapsed.
  • Keep a simple log of every removal request.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I completely remove my personal information from the internet?

Usually not completely. You can remove a large share of what strangers see by opting out of people-search sites and data brokers, removing Google results and deleting old accounts. Breached data, public records and news stories are much harder to remove.

How do I remove my personal information from Google?

Use Results about you for your address, phone or email, and Google's removal form for ID numbers, financial details or doxxing. Google hides the result, but the page stays online, so also ask the website to remove it.

How do I remove my information from data brokers?

Use each broker's free opt-out form, starting with the biggest people-search sites. California residents can file one DROP request for every registered broker. Our data broker removal guide lists 25 sites with their opt-out steps.

Can I remove my personal information for free?

Yes. Opt-out forms, DROP, Google's removal tools and legal deletion requests are all free. Paid services save you time rather than unlock anything you cannot do yourself.

How long does it take to remove personal information?

Most people-search opt-outs take effect within a few days. Legal requests can take up to a month in the UK, and DROP works in 45-day cycles. Plan for a few weeks for a first cleanup, then quarterly rechecks.

Does deleting social media remove my information from the internet?

It reduces exposure, but copies, screenshots and data collected earlier may still exist. Delete the account, then search for yourself again to check what remains.

Why does my personal information come back after I remove it?

Brokers refresh their data from public records and from each other, so a new address or phone number can create a new listing. That is why a three-monthly recheck, or a service that rechecks for you, matters.
