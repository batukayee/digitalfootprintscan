Qantas Breach: A Phone Call to a Call Centre Agent

No password was guessed and no firewall was broken. A caller pretended to be company IT, and a helpful agent followed the instructions.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Australia's privacy regulator (OAIC) published a report on July 16, 2026 on its preliminary inquiries into the Qantas data breach. About 5.67 million customer records were compromised after a threat actor phoned an agent at an overseas contact centre posing as "Qantas IT help." The OAIC closed its inquiries without regulatory action. See what an exposed email address means.

Call centres are built to help, and that is exactly what makes them a target. The Qantas case shows how a convincing voice on the phone can open a customer database without any technical attack at all.

What happened

According to the OAIC, access occurred on June 28, 2025, the breach was detected on June 30 and Qantas disclosed it on July 2, 2025. About 5.67 million customer records were compromised, though the foreword refers to approximately 5.12 million Australians.

The OAIC ran preliminary inquiries from July 11, 2025 to June 1, 2026 and published its report on July 16, 2026.

How it worked

A threat actor phoned an agent at an overseas contact centre run by a third-party provider, posing as "Qantas IT help." The steps they guided the agent through connected the agent's customer relationship management (CRM) instance to the attacker's data extraction tool. The OAIC calls this vishing, which is phishing by voice.

The agent was not hacked. The agent was talked into doing the connecting.

What was exposed

About 4 million records held names, phone numbers, email addresses and Frequent Flyer details. About 1.7 million records had more: addresses, dates of birth, gender or meal preferences. Qantas says credit card details, financial information, passport details and passwords or PINs were not compromised.

Even without payment data, that information is useful to scammers. A message that knows a person's name, travel history and Frequent Flyer details can sound very convincing.

How it ended

The OAIC found no information that Qantas failed to take reasonable steps and closed its preliminary inquiries without an investigation or regulatory action. It stresses that its findings are preliminary and not concluded, and that the Commissioner may still investigate later.

For customers, there is no compensation described in the report, and the data cannot be recalled.

A timeline of the case

The sequence was fast. Access occurred on June 28, 2025, Qantas detected the breach two days later on June 30 and disclosed it on July 2. The OAIC began preliminary inquiries on July 11, 2025 and closed them on June 1, 2026, and the report appeared on July 16, 2026.

The speed of disclosure, within days, contrasts with the year of regulatory work that followed.

Key facts at a glance

  • Records compromised: about 5.67 million
  • Access, detection and disclosure: June 28, June 30 and July 2, 2025
  • Method: a call to a contact-centre agent posing as IT help
  • Data in about 4 million records: names, phone numbers, email addresses and Frequent Flyer details
  • Data in about 1.7 million records: addresses, dates of birth, gender or meal preferences
  • OAIC outcome: inquiries closed without regulatory action; not concluded findings

Why this case matters

The OAIC's decision does not mean customers were unaffected. It reflects its view on what it could establish at the preliminary stage.

The attack path is the lesson. A third-party contact centre with strong technical controls can still be undone by one persuasive call. Training and verification of internal requests matter as much as software.

What vishing means for ordinary callers

Vishing is simply phishing by phone. The attacker uses a confident voice and a plausible role, such as IT help, to get a person to take an action. The target in this case was an agent, but the same technique is used on customers, who receive calls from a "bank" or "technology support."

The shared defense is a rule that does not depend on judging the caller's tone: use only official channels you initiate yourself.
