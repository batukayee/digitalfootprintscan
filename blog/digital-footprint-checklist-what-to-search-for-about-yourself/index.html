Digital Footprint Checklist: What to Search For About Yourself

A self-audit is only as good as the searches you run. This checklist covers every identifier and every place worth checking, and how to keep a record.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 26, 2026
TL;DR

Search your name variations, emails, phone numbers, usernames, home addresses and photos, on Google, Bing, people-search sites and the social networks you use. Check breach data separately. Record each find in a simple inventory with the source, the risk and the action, then fix the highest-risk items first and repeat every few months.

Most people search their own name once, see a few familiar results, and stop. That misses most of what a determined stranger, a recruiter or a scammer would find. A proper self-audit searches every identifier you use, in every place that matters, and records what turns up so you can act on it.

Use this checklist as a working document. Run it in a private browser window, signed out of your accounts, so results are not personalised to you.

Part 1: Identifiers to search

1Your name, in every form

Search your full name in quotes, then add variations: with and without middle name or initial, nicknames, maiden or previous names, and common misspellings. Add your city, employer or school to narrow common names. See what people can find from your name.

2Every email address you have used

Search each one in quotes, including old school, work and ISP addresses. Our guide to checking if your email is public lists extra search patterns.

3Every phone number

Search current and past numbers in several formats, such as with dashes, spaces and the country code. See our phone number exposure check.

4Usernames and handles

Search each handle you have used for gaming, forums or social media. A shared handle is one of the easiest ways to link accounts. See username search.

5Home addresses, current and past

Search each address in quotes. Results that pair it with your name matter most. See removing your name and address.

6Your photos

Run a reverse image search on your main profile photos to see where else they appear. See removing photos of yourself from Google.

Part 2: Where to look

  • Google and Bing: check web, image and news results. Bing often shows different pages.
  • Search operators: add site: to search one site, filetype:pdf for documents, and quotes for exact matches. See searching Google for your personal information.
  • People-search sites: open the listings that appear for your name and note each one. See people-search sites.
  • Social networks: search for yourself while logged out, and check what your profiles show to strangers.
  • Breach data: run a free email breach check. Search engines do not show this.
  • Archives: look up old personal sites or profiles on the Wayback Machine.

Part 3: Check how your identifiers connect

The most revealing results are often not single pages but links between them: the same username on a gaming profile and a professional site, the same phone number on a resume and a people-search listing, or the same photo on two accounts you thought were separate. As you search, note which identifiers appear together. Our guide on checking whether your identifiers are linked shows how to test this deliberately.

Part 4: See your accounts as a stranger does

  • Facebook and Instagram: use the "view as" or public preview options, or check from a logged-out browser.
  • LinkedIn: check your public profile view, which is what search engines and non-members see.
  • X, TikTok and YouTube: open your profile in a private window and scroll back through older posts.
  • Payment and fitness apps: check whether transactions or activities are public; see Venmo privacy.

Part 5: Record what you find

A list of links is not enough. For each result, record five things in a simple sheet or note:

  1. Where: the exact URL and the site name.
  2. What: which details it shows, such as name, address, phone number or photo.
  3. Who controls it: you, a third-party site, a people-search site or a search engine only.
  4. Risk: high for home address, phone number, ID numbers and children's details; medium for employer, relatives and email; low for professional profiles you want public.
  5. Action and date: edit, delete, opt out, request removal or keep, plus the date to recheck.

This becomes your personal digital footprint inventory. Next time, you are checking for changes rather than starting again.

Keep the sheet somewhere private, such as an encrypted note or a password manager's secure notes, because it gathers your personal details in one place. Do not paste it into public documents or shared drives.

How long a full audit takes

The first time, set aside two to three hours: about an hour for searches, an hour for social profiles and breach checks, and the rest for recording results. Later checks take 15 to 30 minutes, because you only look for changes against your inventory.

Part 6: Fix in order of risk

Do first

Anything showing your home address, phone number, ID numbers or children's details, and any breached password still in use.

Do next

Old accounts, public posts you would not write today, and people-search listings without your address.

For people-search listings, use our opt-out routine. For pages showing your contact details, Google's Results about you tool can request removal from search and alert you to new matches; it does not delete the page itself. For forgotten accounts, see finding old accounts.

If a search turns up something upsetting, such as your home address on a site you do not recognise, slow down and work through it methodically. Extra checks for specific situations

Before a job search

Look at your results as a recruiter would. Check that your professional profiles are current and consistent, set personal profiles to private, and review public posts and comments under your real name. Check that your LinkedIn shows what you intend; see LinkedIn privacy settings.

After a move or a breakup

Search your new address, update or hide it where you can, and recheck people-search sites a few months later, when new listings tend to appear. See auditing after a major life change and privacy after a separation.

Before a first date or meeting strangers

Assume the other person will search you. See what people can find before a first date.

The checklist at a glance

  1. Name in quotes, plus variations, city, employer and school.
  2. Every email address, current and old, in quotes and with filetype:pdf.
  3. Every phone number in several formats.
  4. Every username or handle.
  5. Current and past home addresses in quotes.
  6. Reverse image search on profile photos.
  7. Google web, images and news, then Bing.
  8. People-search listings for your name and address.
  9. Logged-out view of each social profile.
  10. Free breach check for each email address.
  11. Record each find: URL, details, owner, risk, action, date.
  12. Fix high-risk items first, then set alerts and a recheck date.

If a listing appears on a site you do not recognise, our guide to identifying which broker a listing comes from helps you find the company behind it.

Set up ongoing monitoring

Create a Google Alert for your name in quotes and for your main email address, so new mentions are emailed to you. Turn on alerts in Google's Results about you tool if it is available in your country. Then rerun this checklist every three to six months; see how often to check.

Frequently asked questions

What should I search for about myself?

Your name and its variations, every email address and phone number you have used, your usernames, your home addresses and your profile photos.

Should I search while logged in?

No. Use a private window and stay signed out, so results reflect what other people see.

How do I keep track of what I find?

Record the URL, what it shows, who controls it, the risk level, and the action and date. That record becomes your inventory for future checks.

What should I fix first?

Anything with your home address, phone number, ID numbers or children's details, and any leaked password you still use.

How often should I repeat the checklist?

Every three to six months, and after a move, a job change, a breakup or a major data breach.

Do I need paid tools to check my digital footprint?

No. Search engines, a free breach check and the opt-out pages of people-search sites cover most of it. Paid tools mainly save time and add ongoing monitoring.
