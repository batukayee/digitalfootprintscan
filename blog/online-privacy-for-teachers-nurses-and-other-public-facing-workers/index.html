Privacy for Teachers, Nurses and Public-Facing Workers

If your job puts you in front of the public, strangers can find you with a name and a workplace. A few habits reduce what they find.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Teachers, nurses and similar workers are identifiable by name, employer and licensing records, which makes them easier to locate than most. CISA says doxxing can be used for spear-phishing, identity theft and account takeover, and advises leaving birthdate and location off profiles. California's Safe at Home program covers some public employees and health care workers who fear for their safety. Start by checking your listings and keeping home and work details separate.

Anyone whose job involves the public can be found by name. A teacher is listed on a school website. A nurse is licensed under a public register. A parent, patient or stranger with a grievance can sit down with a search box and start. Most people never will, but a few steps lower the risk if someone does.

Why these roles are easier to find

A few features make public-facing workers visible:

  • Staff pages and school or hospital directories list names and roles
  • Licensing boards publish names and sometimes addresses, covered in professional license directories
  • Professional social pages show employer and city
  • News stories and local events name staff members
  • People-search sites connect a name to a home address

Put together, a workplace and a name often lead to a house.

What CISA and the EFF say

CISA notes that doxxing, which is publishing someone's private information, can enable spear-phishing, identity theft and account takeover. Its advice includes making accounts private, leaving your birthdate and location off profiles, turning off geo-tags, controlling tagging and reviewing third-party apps. The EFF's doxxing guide recommends checking breach databases, requesting removal from open-records mirror sites, using address confidentiality programs, making social accounts private and treating manual broker opt-outs as the most effective route.

Separate work and home. Use an employer address for work contact, a different email for personal accounts, and a profile photo and handle that are not used on dating, family or neighborhood pages.

Special protections

Some roles have formal protections. California's Safe at Home program provides a substitute mailing address and says eligible people include victims of domestic violence, stalking and trafficking, certain health care workers and patients, and public employees who fear for their safety. Check eligibility with your state. Federal judges and their families have additional protection under the Daniel Anderl Judicial Security and Privacy Act, which does not cover other workers.

A practical checklist

1Search your name with your workplace

Search your full name plus your school, hospital or city. Note which pages show a home address, a family member or a personal social profile.

2Check people-search sites

Look for listings with your address and relatives. Follow the people-search opt-out guide for removal.

3Lock down personal social accounts

Make accounts private, remove your workplace from personal bios and ask friends not to tag your location or school.

4Use a work-only contact

Give parents and the public a work email address or school contact, not a personal phone number.

5Review licenses and registrations

Check the address on your license, voter registration and any business filings. Use a P.O. box or alternate address where your board allows it.

6Ask your employer what it publishes

Find out whether staff directories or newsletters list home details, and ask for changes.

If you are targeted

If someone has published your information or is harassing you, preserve evidence by taking screenshots and noting URLs, then report it to the platform and your employer. Read what is doxxing and how to protect yourself. If you are in danger, contact the police.

Boundaries with students, patients and parents

Keep professional contact on professional channels. Check your employer's social media policy, and think before accepting friend requests from students, patients or their families. A professional account for work updates keeps the public conversation away from your personal profile. If someone contacts you on a personal account, move the conversation to an official channel and tell your employer where appropriate. These habits protect your privacy, and they also protect the people you serve.

Frequently asked questions

Why are teachers and nurses more exposed?

Their names and workplaces are often public, and their licenses may list addresses, making it easier to connect a name to a home.

Can I hide my address from my licensing board?

Some boards allow an alternate public address. California boards describe P.O. box options and often still require a confidential street address.

What is Safe at Home?

A California program that provides a substitute mailing address for eligible people, including some health care workers and public employees who fear for their safety.

Should I stay off social media?

Not necessarily. Make personal accounts private, avoid workplace details and keep location off.

What if my address is on a people-search site?

Opt out of the listing. The address removal guide covers the process.
