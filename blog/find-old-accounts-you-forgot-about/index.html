How to Find Old Accounts, Usernames and Posts You Forgot About

Every account you forgot is a password that may have leaked and a profile someone can still read. Here is how to find them all, and what to do next.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 26, 2026
TL;DR

Search your email inboxes for welcome and verification messages, review saved passwords in your browser or password manager, check apps connected to your Google, Apple and Facebook logins, and check breach data. Then search your old usernames to find profiles, posts and comments. Delete what you do not need, and secure what you keep with a unique password.

Your email may already be in a known data breach. Find out in about 30 seconds.

Most people have dozens of accounts they no longer remember: an old forum, a shopping site used once, a social network that went quiet, a gaming profile from school. Each one can still hold your name, email, birthday, photos or posts. And if the site is breached, the password you used there may unlock accounts you care about.

This guide combines the methods that actually work, from fastest to slowest, and covers old usernames, social profiles, and posts or comments as well as accounts.

Method 1: Search your email inboxes

Almost every account starts with a confirmation email. Search each inbox you have ever used, including old ones, for terms such as welcome, verify your email, confirm your account, your account, password reset and unsubscribe. Sort by oldest first. Each sender is a likely account. Do not forget old school, work and ISP addresses; if you can still log in to them, search them too.

Method 2: Check saved passwords

Your browser or password manager is a list of accounts you have logged in to. Open the saved passwords list in Chrome, Safari, Edge, Firefox or your password manager and export or review it. Many managers also flag passwords that are reused or appear in known breaches, which tells you which accounts to deal with first.

Method 3: Review "sign in with" connections

If you used a social or platform login, the account may never have had its own password, so it will not appear in your password list.

1Google

Open your Google Account linked apps page to see apps using Sign in with Google and apps with access to your account. Google explains how to stop using Sign in with Google for an app and how to remove access (Google Account Help). Stopping the sign-in does not delete your data in that app.

2Apple

On iPhone, open Settings, tap your name, then Sign in with Apple; on the web, sign in to your Apple Account and open Sign-In & Security (Apple Support). If you used Hide My Email, the same area shows which apps have a forwarding address.

3Facebook and others

In Facebook settings, the Apps and websites section lists services you logged into with Facebook. Check X, Microsoft and any other account you used as a login too.

Method 4: Check breach data

A breach check lists services that leaked your email, which often reveals accounts you forgot. Run a free email breach check for each address. Remember that Have I Been Pwned only knows a subset of breaches: a clean result is not proof that the address never leaked. If you find breaches, see whether an old breach still matters.

Method 5: Search your old usernames

Usernames find what email searches miss: forum profiles, gaming accounts and comments. Make a list of every handle you have used, then search each one in quotes on Google and Bing, with and without site names. Many people reused one handle for years, which makes it easy for anyone to connect those accounts. See what people can find from a username and how a Reddit username can be linked to you.

Method 6: Find old social media accounts

Search each social network for your name, old usernames and old email addresses while logged out. Most networks offer account recovery by email or phone; if you can recover an old account, you can delete it properly. Our guide to finding social accounts by email shows what others can see.

Method 7: Find old posts and comments

  • Use each platform's own data download or activity log to see your past posts, likes and comments in one place.
  • Search your name or username together with the site name on Google, for example "oldhandle" site:example.com.
  • Check archived versions of your old pages on the Wayback Machine. The Internet Archive accepts exclusion requests at info@archive.org with the URLs and time period (Internet Archive Help).

Method 8: Check old phone numbers

Accounts tied to a phone number you no longer have are a security risk, because mobile numbers are often reassigned to new customers. If the new owner receives your login codes or password resets, they could take over the account. List any number you have given up, then check your important accounts' recovery settings and replace it. See what to do about an old phone number that is still public.

Method 9: Look for linked identifiers

Once you have a list, compare it. An old username, email and phone number that appear together on one site can lead someone to all your other accounts. Our guide on checking whether your identifiers are linked explains how to spot these connections.

Services that shut down or changed owners

Some forgotten accounts belong to services that closed, merged or were sold. The data may have moved to the new owner with a new privacy policy. If you find a welcome email from a service that no longer exists, search for who acquired it and whether your account was migrated. Breach checks are useful here too: data from closed services sometimes surfaces in breaches years later.

Accounts you set up for others

Parents often create accounts for children, and partners share streaming or shopping logins. List any account you created for someone else, decide who should own it, and move it to their email or close it. Shared logins with a reused password are a common route for account takeovers.

Prioritise what to fix first

  1. Accounts that use a password you still use elsewhere, or that appear in a breach.
  2. Accounts holding payment details, ID documents or your home address.
  3. Public profiles and posts under your real name.
  4. Dormant accounts with little data, which you can delete in a batch later.

What to do with each account you find

Delete it

If you do not need it, use the site's delete-account option, not just "deactivate". Remove personal details first if deletion is slow or unclear.

Keep and secure it

Change to a unique password, turn on two-step verification and update the recovery email and phone number.

If a site has no deletion option, remove what you can from the profile, change the email to an alias, and ask for deletion in writing. Where data protection law applies, cite your rights: see US deletion requests and the GDPR right to erasure. For a deleted page that still shows in Google, use Refresh outdated content.

Prevent forgotten accounts in future

Use a password manager so every new account is recorded automatically, use email aliases so you can see which service each address belongs to, and review your connected apps every few months. For a full clean-up plan, see cleaning up your old online presence.

Frequently asked questions

How can I find all accounts linked to my email?

Search the inbox for welcome and verification emails, review saved passwords, check sign-in connections in Google, Apple and Facebook, and run a breach check for the address.

Can I find accounts linked to an email I no longer use?

Yes, if you can still access it. If not, breach checks and username searches can still reveal accounts, and many sites let you recover access through a phone number.

Is deactivating an account the same as deleting it?

No. Deactivation usually hides the profile but keeps the data. Use the delete-account option if you want the data removed.

How do I find my old posts and comments?

Use the platform's data download or activity log, and search your old usernames with the site name on Google.

Why do forgotten accounts matter?

They hold personal data, they can be breached, and a reused password from one of them can be used to break into your other accounts.

Can I get a list of every account linked to my phone number?

There is no single list. Search your text messages for verification codes, check the recovery settings of your main accounts, and look at sign-in connections and saved passwords.
