Should You Worry About an Old Data Breach?

Time makes some breaches harmless and others permanent. The question is which data leaked and whether you changed anything since.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Worry less if the breach exposed only an email and you have not reused that password. Act if it exposed a password you still use anywhere, or data you cannot change, such as a date of birth, home address, phone number or government ID. Check your current passwords, enable multi-factor authentication and watch for phishing that uses the old data.

Many people find their email in a breach from ten years ago and wonder whether it is still worth acting on. The honest answer is: sometimes. Age matters less than what leaked and what has changed since.

Why old breaches can still matter

Some data does not expire. Dates of birth, former and current addresses, phone numbers and government IDs remain useful long after a breach. Passwords also matter if you reused them, because attackers test old lists against new sites. OWASP notes that many users re-use the same password and username or email, which is what makes credential stuffing work. See credential stuffing explained.

Old breaches also keep circulating. A collection stolen years ago can be combined with newer ones to build fuller profiles.

When it is probably fine

  • The breach exposed only an email address or username.
  • You have never reused the breached password and no longer use that service.
  • You have unique passwords and multi-factor authentication on key accounts.
  • The data was of a type that is out of date, such as an old address you no longer use.

Even then, expect some spam and be cautious about messages that mention the old service.

When to take action

  • The breach exposed a password and you reused it, even in a modified form.
  • It included a date of birth, address, phone number or national ID that is still yours.
  • It included financial details tied to accounts you still hold.
  • Your email appears in many breaches and is also used for important accounts.

How to check quickly

1List the breaches and data types

Use a breach lookup to see which breaches your address appears in, with dates and data types. Our guide to how to check if your email address has been exposed explains it.

2Test your current passwords

Pwned Passwords checks whether a password appears in known breach data. It hashes your password on your side and sends only the first five characters of the hash. Google's Password Checkup, which Google recommends, offers a similar check inside Google Password Manager.

3Replace anything reused or exposed

Change passwords that match old ones. The FTC advises changing the password for the affected company and for any account with a similar one.

4Secure the accounts that matter most

Email, banking and any account that can reset others should have unique passwords and multi-factor authentication.

5Decide on credit steps

If government ID numbers or financial data were involved, consider a credit freeze.

What you cannot undo

You cannot erase a breach, and you cannot make a criminal forget your data. You also cannot easily change a date of birth. Public records and old data broker profiles can reappear because new sources keep feeding them. For that reason, see why data broker profiles contain old information.

Absence is not proof. Have I Been Pwned itself states that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A clean result does not mean you were never exposed.

A simple decision rule

1. Did it include a password you ever reused? Change it everywhere. 2. Did it include data you cannot change? Reduce what can be done with it by freezing credit and tightening account recovery. 3. Neither? Add alerts, move on and keep your habits strong.

A simple way to judge an old breach

In our view, three questions decide whether an old breach still matters. First, did it include a password you have reused anywhere? If so, change it now. Second, was the exposed data something that can be changed, such as a password, or something that cannot, such as a date of birth? Fixed details stay useful to criminals for years. Third, are you seeing signs of misuse, like unfamiliar accounts, odd texts or credit inquiries you did not make? A breach from a decade ago with no reused password and no signs of misuse is usually a lower priority than a recent one.

Frequently asked questions

Does an old breach expire?

No. Some data stays useful for years, especially identifiers that rarely change.

Should I delete the old account?

If you still have access, yes. It reduces future exposure, though it does not remove data already copied.

How do I know if the breach was real?

Breach databases mark entries as unverified or fabricated when that applies. HIBP says an unverified breach may not have been hacked from the indicated website.

Is it worth contacting the company?

It can help you close the account or ask what data it holds. See the guides on your data rights for GDPR and CCPA.

Will changing my password help if the old password was hashed?

Yes, if you still use it anywhere. Weak hashed passwords can sometimes be recovered.
