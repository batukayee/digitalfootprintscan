Blackmail Emails Claiming to Know Your Password

An email quotes a password you once used and threatens to expose you. Here is how to respond.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 26, 2026
TL;DR

Blackmail emails that include one of your real passwords and threaten to release embarrassing material unless you pay are a recognised scam. The FTC’s alert says not to pay, to delete the message, to change the password if you still use it and to report the email. A leaked password often comes from an old data breach rather than from a hack of your device. Check whether your email appeared in breaches and turn on two-factor authentication.

Scammers start with details that have already leaked. Is your email one of them?

Seeing your own password in a threatening email is frightening, and that is the point. This guide follows the FTC’s consumer alert on blackmail emails of this kind.

What the email says

The message usually claims that the sender has hacked your device, recorded you or gained access to private material. To make the claim look real, it includes a password you have used. It then demands payment, typically in cryptocurrency, and threatens to share the material with your contacts if you do not pay.

What the FTC advises

The FTC’s alert gives clear steps:

  • Do not pay. Paying does not guarantee silence and can lead to more demands.
  • Delete the email. Do not reply or click any links.
  • Change the password shown in the email, if you still use it, and change it anywhere else you have used it.
  • Report the scam to the FTC.

Why the scammer knows your password

The sender usually does not need to have hacked you. Passwords leaked in past data breaches circulate widely, and scammers can pair them with email addresses. If an old password appears in the message, it may come from a service that was breached years ago. Signs it is a mass scam

  • The message is generic and sent to many people.
  • It gives no specific proof of the material it claims to hold.
  • It sets a deadline and demands payment in cryptocurrency.
  • The sender’s address is spoofed or unrelated.

Scammers rely on fear. Take a breath before you act.

What to do next

  1. Change the password everywhere you used it, with a unique new one for each account.
  2. Use a password manager to create and store unique passwords.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication, preferably with an authenticator app.
  4. Check your exposure. A dark web or breach scan shows where your details have appeared. See dark web scan and checking whether your email address is public.
  5. Cover your webcam if it makes you feel better, but know that the typical email makes false claims.
  6. Tell someone you trust if you feel threatened.
  7. Keep a copy of the message and report it.

If the threat is real

If the sender names specific material that you recognise, or threatens violence, treat it more seriously. Contact local police, keep evidence and do not send money. Reporting to the authorities helps.

Related threats

Breach data can also lead to extortion by text or phone; see the Optus data blackmail case. Scammers who demand payment may ask for crypto machines or gift cards; see Bitcoin ATM scams. To learn how scammers find your contact details, read this guide, and for scale see scam loss statistics.

Key point. A real password in a blackmail email usually comes from an old breach. Do not pay, delete the email, change the password and turn on two-factor authentication.

The newer version: a photo of your home

In 2024 KrebsOnSecurity described a variant that adds a personal touch. The email opens with the recipient's full name and includes a photo of their street or front yard, along with their address. It arrived as a two-page PDF, claimed spyware had recorded the recipient through their webcam, and demanded just under $2,000 in Bitcoin through a QR code, with a 24-hour deadline (KrebsOnSecurity).

The photo is meant to frighten you, but KrebsOnSecurity reports that the images appear to come from online mapping services. A scammer who has your name and address from a breach or a people-search site can find a picture of your street in seconds without ever being near it. It is the same mass scam with a new detail. If your home address is easy to find with your name, see how to remove your name and address from the internet.

Where to report in the US

As well as reporting to the FTC, you can file a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov or contact your local FBI field office. The Arizona Attorney General's warning about these emails points to the same FBI channels, and advises not to respond, not to engage and not to pay (Arizona Attorney General).

Frequently asked questions

Should I pay a blackmail email?

No. The FTC advises not to pay.

Why does the email include my real password?

Passwords leaked in past data breaches circulate widely, and scammers can pair them with email addresses.

What should I do with the email?

Delete it, do not reply or click links, and report it.

Do I need to change my password?

Yes, if you still use the password shown, and anywhere else you have used it.

How can I check my exposure?

Use a breach or dark web scan and turn on two-factor authentication.

Why does the blackmail email have a photo of my house?

Newer versions add a picture of your street, which appears to come from online mapping services. It does not mean anyone has been to your home.

Where can I report a sextortion email in the US?

Report it to the FTC and to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov.
