What Are Data Brokers, What Do They Collect, and Why Do They Have My Information?

Data brokers are companies you have never dealt with that still know your address, your age and a lot more. Here is how the industry works, in plain English.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 26, 2026
TL;DR

Data brokers collect personal information from public records, purchases, apps, websites and other brokers, then sell or license it for marketing, risk checks and people-search reports. They also make guesses about you, such as income or interests. Old data lingers because records are kept and resold. You can opt out broker by broker, and in some places you have a legal right to deletion.

A data broker is a company that collects information about people and sells or licenses it to others. The defining feature is that you usually have no direct relationship with the broker. You did not sign up, buy anything or agree to anything with them, yet they may hold a file with your name, addresses, phone numbers and a long list of interests and estimates.

This guide explains how the industry works: the main types of brokers, what they collect, the inferences they make, why their data is often years out of date, and what you can do about it.

What the FTC found when it studied data brokers

The clearest official look at the industry is still the FTC's 2014 study of nine data brokers: Acxiom, CoreLogic, Datalogix, eBureau, ID Analytics, Intelius, PeekYou, Rapleaf and Recorded Future. According to the FTC's announcement on May 27, 2014:

  • the brokers held information on nearly every US consumer;
  • one broker's database held information on more than 1.4 billion consumer transactions and over 700 billion aggregated data elements, and another added more than 3 billion new data points each month;
  • data came from online and offline sources, largely without consumers' knowledge;
  • seven of the nine brokers shared information with another broker in the study;
  • consumer choices about this data were, in the FTC's words, "largely invisible and incomplete".

The figures are from 2014, so treat them as a floor rather than today's size. For more recent numbers, see our data broker and people-search statistics.

The three main types of data brokers

Marketing brokers

Build audience segments and profiles so companies can target ads and mail. You rarely see these files, but they shape the ads and offers you get.

Risk and identity brokers

Sell identity verification and fraud-prevention data to banks, insurers and retailers. Some of this activity is covered by credit-reporting law.

The third type is the one most people meet first: people-search sites, which sell look-up reports to anyone. The FTC describes these as a type of data broker. They are covered in our guide to people-search sites.

What information data brokers collect

Every broker is different, but files commonly include:

  • Identity and contact data: names and past names, current and previous addresses, phone numbers, email addresses and date of birth or age.
  • Household data: relatives, people who share your address, estimated household size and home ownership.
  • Public-record data: property records, voter information, court records and licences. See what counts as a public record.
  • Purchase and behaviour data: the FTC report cites purchase history and social media activity among the data collected.
  • Device and online data: advertising IDs, cookies and browsing signals. See tracking cookies and browser fingerprinting.

Inferences: what brokers guess about you

Brokers do not only store facts. They create categories and predictions, such as likely income band, interests or life events. The FTC gave an example of a segment called "Biker Enthusiasts": it could help a motorcycle shop offer discounts, but an insurer could also read it as a sign of risky behaviour. The report also flagged inferences about ethnicity, income, religion and health.

These guesses can be wrong and you usually cannot see them. That is one reason privacy laws increasingly treat inferences as personal data that you can ask to access or delete.

Why broker profiles contain old information

If a broker file shows an address you left ten years ago, a maiden name or a long-cancelled phone number, there are usually simple reasons:

1Records are added to, not replaced

When a new address appears, the old one usually stays in the file as history. Public records such as deeds and court filings keep past addresses permanently.

2Data is resold in copies

Brokers buy files from each other. A copy sold years ago may never receive your later updates, so the old version keeps circulating.

3Some brokers keep data indefinitely

The FTC noted that some brokers store data indefinitely, and warned this could create security risks.

4Matching mistakes stick

If your file was once merged with someone who has a similar name, their details can stay attached until someone removes them.

Old data is not harmless. A past address can still lead someone to family members, and old phone numbers and emails are used in scams and account recovery attacks. For the related problem on people-search sites, see why old info and relatives keep appearing.

Where data brokers get your information

The FTC found brokers use a mix of online and offline sources. In practice that includes government records, retailers and loyalty programmes, warranty and survey forms, apps and websites that share data with partners, and other brokers. A useful mental model is that any time you hand over details to a company, ask whether its privacy policy allows sharing with "partners" or "third parties". If it does, the data may end up with a broker.

How to tell which broker has your data

You will never see most broker files directly, but there are clues. People-search listings show up in search results, and many name their parent company in the footer or privacy policy; our guide to identifying which broker a listing comes from shows how to trace them. Targeted mail and ads that use an old name or address suggest a marketing file. Genealogy-style sites such as FamilyTreeNow and large risk brokers such as LexisNexis often hold the oldest records. Where you have access rights, a formal request will show you exactly what a company holds.

Why your information keeps coming back

Deleting a broker file does not stop the flow that created it. New public records, new purchases and new app sign-ups keep feeding fresh data into the system, and a broker that deleted your file can rebuild it from a source it buys from later. That is why removal is a cycle rather than a one-off. Our guide to why personal information keeps appearing online explains the loop, and reducing your footprint covers how to slow it down.

Your rights over data broker files

  • California: the CCPA gives rights to know, delete and opt out of the sale of personal information (California Attorney General). The state's DROP platform lets residents send one free deletion request to all registered brokers; from August 1, 2026, brokers must process requests at least every 45 days. See our guide to DROP.
  • Other US states: several states, including Texas, Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut and Oregon, give residents deletion and opt-out rights.
  • UK and EU: you can make a subject access request to see what a company holds and use the right to erasure.
  • Canada: see PIPEDA access and deletion requests.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal for data brokers to sell my information?

In the US, generally yes, with limits. Reports used for hiring, housing, credit or insurance must follow credit-reporting rules, and a growing number of states give you rights to delete or opt out.

How do I find out which data brokers have my information?

Search your name for people-search listings, and use access or deletion requests where your state or country gives you that right. California residents can reach all registered brokers at once through DROP.

Can I remove myself from every data broker?

Not completely. You can remove yourself from most visible ones, but files are resold and new brokers appear. Regular rechecks keep exposure low.

Are data brokers the same as credit bureaus?

No, although some companies do both. Credit bureaus produce credit reports under strict rules. Many data broker products are not credit reports.

Why do data brokers have information I never shared?

They buy it from other companies, collect it from public records, or infer it from patterns in your data.
