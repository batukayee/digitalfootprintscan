Is Voter Registration Public? What Your Record Reveals

Registering to vote can put your name and address on lists that others can obtain. The rules depend on the state, so check yours.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

In the United States, voter registration data is not uniformly public. Some states let any person request the voter file, while others limit it to parties, campaigns or officials. Most files include names, addresses, party and voting history, and states generally withhold Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers and full dates of birth. Address confidentiality programs exist for people at risk.

Registering to vote is a civic act, but it also creates a record. People are often surprised to learn that some of that record can be requested by others, and that it can end up on people-search sites. The picture is more nuanced than "all voter data is public".

Voter data is governed by state rules

The National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) says states vary on who can request voter lists. In some states anyone can ask. Michigan, for example, allows any person to request the voter file. In others, access is restricted. Alabama limits it to parties and legislators, and Maine to parties, get-out-the-vote organizations, officeholders, government and law enforcement.

Many states charge a fee, and some restrict how the data may be used, for example to political or non-commercial purposes.

What a voter file usually contains

According to NCSL, most files include:

  • Your name
  • Your address
  • Party affiliation, in states that record it
  • Voting history, meaning whether you voted in past elections, not how

Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers and full dates of birth are generally withheld. Those fields, which are most useful for identity theft, are usually protected even where the rest is accessible.

Why it still ends up online

Even in states with restrictions, lists reach parties and campaigns, and some data can be obtained for permitted purposes. People-search sites can then reuse records that are available to them. A listing might show a home address, an age range or relatives, which is why voter data is a common source for people-search listings. Public does not always mean easy. In some states you must apply for the list and agree to use limits. In practice, the risk is that data obtained legitimately later circulates beyond its intended use.

Who the data matters most for

  • People with stalkers, abusive ex-partners or harassing contacts
  • Public-facing workers such as teachers, nurses and officials. See online privacy for public-facing workers.
  • Anyone who wants to keep a home address out of searches

How to limit exposure

1Check your state's rules

Look at your state election office's page for who can obtain the voter list, what fields are included and whether you can register with a confidential address.

2Consider an Address Confidentiality Program

NCSL says participants in address confidentiality programs are protected in nearly every state. California's Safe at Home program, run by the Secretary of State, provides a substitute mailing address for eligible people such as victims of domestic violence, stalking and certain other threats.

3Ask your election office about confidential registration

Some offices can place a protected status on records for people with safety concerns. Ask directly what they allow.

4Remove copies from people-search sites

Opting out of broker listings reduces what appears on searches, though sources can refeed them. See how to remove your address from the internet.

5Re-check periodically

New registrations and updates can re-create records, so check again later.

What not to do

Do not avoid registering to vote because of this. Registration is important for civic participation, and there are protections for people who need them. If you are worried, speak to your local election office about options before you register or update your record.

Voter data versus other public records

Voter files are one of several record types. Property deeds, court filings and business registrations may also show an address. Read how property records make your home address public for the next source to check.

Frequently asked questions

Does voter data show how I voted?

No. Files show whether you voted in past elections, not your choices.

Can anyone download my voter record?

It depends on your state. Some states allow any person to request the file, and others restrict access.

Is my Social Security number in the voter file?

NCSL says Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers and full dates of birth are generally withheld.

Can I remove myself from the voter file?

Registered voters cannot simply delete a record while staying registered. Ask your election office about confidential registration or address confidentiality programs.

Why does my voter address appear on people-search sites?

Records available for permitted uses can be copied by data brokers. See what information people-search sites show.
