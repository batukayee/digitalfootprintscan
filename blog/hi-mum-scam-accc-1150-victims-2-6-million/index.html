"Hi Mum" Scam: 1,150 Victims and $2.6 Million Lost

The message says "Hi Mum, I've lost my phone." It comes from an unknown number, and the person asking for money is not your child.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) said on August 23, 2022 that more than 1,150 Australians had fallen victim to "Hi Mum" scams in the first seven months of 2022, with reported losses of $2.6 million. Not every incident has a criminal case. Sometimes the story is a pattern that a regulator measures and warns about. The "Hi Mum" scam is a good example of a pattern that is easy to describe and hard to spot when it arrives on your phone.

What happened

The ACCC reported on August 23, 2022 that more than 1,150 Australians had fallen victim to "Hi Mum" scams in the first seven months of 2022, with reported losses of $2.6 million. Most reports came in June and July.

According to the ACCC, 82% of these scams were reported by people over 55, and they accounted for 95% of reported losses. Women over 55 made more than two-thirds of the reports, with losses of more than $1.4 million.

How it worked

Scammers, most often on WhatsApp, pose as a family member or friend who has lost their phone and is now on a new number. They then ask for money, claiming they cannot access online banking.

The ACCC notes that some messages use the impersonated person's name and appear to have some of their contact details. Victims also gave photos, personal information and bank transfers.

What was exposed

The scammers appear to hold names and contact details, though the ACCC does not explain where they came from. What victims gave up was money and, in some cases, personal information and photos.

A message that uses a real first name feels personal even when it is part of a mass campaign.

How it ended

This is not a case with a clean ending. The figures describe reports to the regulator, which are likely to be lower than the true losses. The warning's practical advice is to call the saved number, ask a question only the real person could answer, never send money unless sure and contact IDCARE if you shared personal information.

A timeline of the data

The ACCC's figures cover the first seven months of 2022, with the biggest numbers of reports in June and July. The release came on August 23, 2022, when the scam was still growing.

The pattern is useful for planning. Scams like this one rise and fall in waves, and a quick public warning at the start of a wave can reach families before they receive the message themselves.

Key facts at a glance

  • Reports in first seven months of 2022: more than 1,150 victims
  • Reported losses: $2.6 million
  • Peak months: June and July
  • Share of reports from people over 55: 82%, accounting for 95% of reported losses
  • Women over 55: more than two-thirds of reports, losses over $1.4 million
  • Release date: August 23, 2022

Why this case matters

It shows how cheap a mass scam can be to run. A message costs almost nothing, and only a small share of recipients need to reply for it to pay. The ACCC's figures show that those who respond often lose significant sums.

The scam also targets a natural instinct: parents want to help. That instinct is why a code word or a call to the saved number works so well as a defense. It gives the real family member a way to prove who they are.

What you can do

1Call the saved number

Do not reply to the new number, and do not send money first.

2Ask a personal question only the real person could answer

Or use an agreed family code word.

3Never send money unless certain

A genuine family member will understand the delay.

4Report and get help

Report to Scamwatch, and contact IDCARE if you shared personal information.

Starting the conversation at home

Because the scam targets families, the best defense is a conversation that costs nothing. Agree with parents, grandparents and children that anyone asking for money from a new number will be verified by a call to the saved number. Choose a code word that is not guessable from social media.

Reassure older relatives that checking is not rude. A real child who has lost a phone will wait ten minutes for a callback, and a scammer will not.

What the release does not say

The ACCC release does not say who the scammers are or where they operate. For a grandparent-style scam that ended in prison terms, see the Canadian call centres case.

Frequently asked questions

What is the "Hi Mum" scam?

A scammer messages, often on WhatsApp, pretending to be a child or relative on a new number after losing their phone, and asks for money.

How much did Australians lose?

The ACCC reported more than 1,150 victims and $2.6 million in losses in the first seven months of 2022.

Who is most affected?

Reports came mostly from people over 55, who accounted for 95% of reported losses.

How can I check a message?

Call the person's saved number, ask a personal question only they can answer, or use an agreed code word.
