Romance Scam Money Mule Case: A Texas 10-Year Sentence

A federal case from Texas shows how romance scammers rely on ordinary bank accounts, and how a quiet intermediary can end up with a long sentence.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

In March 2024 the US Justice Department announced that a Texas woman had been sentenced to 10 years for her role in a romance-scam laundering ring. A jury had convicted her of conspiracy to commit wire fraud and conspiracy to commit money laundering after a seven-day trial. She was ordered to pay more than $2.2 million in restitution. The case is a reminder that anyone who receives and forwards money for an online partner is part of the fraud.

Romance scams are usually described from the victim's side: a stranger, a warm message, a request for money. A federal case from North Texas is useful because it shows the other half of the machine. The people who open bank accounts, receive the money and pass it on are what turn a conversation into a payment. In March 2024 the US Justice Department announced the sentence of one of them.

What happened

According to the Justice Department, a jury convicted a woman from Aubrey, Texas, of one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud and one count of conspiracy to commit money laundering after a seven-day trial in December 2023. The judge sentenced her to 10 years on each count, to run at the same time, and ordered restitution of more than $2.2 million. The court applied an enhancement for obstruction after finding that she had lied under oath.

Four co-defendants were sentenced as well. The release lists 24 months for two of them and 37 months for the other two. IRS Criminal Investigation led the case, which was indicted in September 2021.

How the scheme worked

The release says the fraudsters used fake names and searched dating sites, including Match.com and Zoosk, for targets. They often looked for people who were divorced or widowed. They built trust by promising a long-term relationship and then invented reasons to ask for money. A common story was that the supposed partner was overseas for work and could not reach his own bank accounts.

Victims were told to send money to accounts opened by the defendants in the Northern District of Texas. The woman passed the proceeds on and kept a portion for herself. That detail matters. The scammer overseas needs a domestic account because a US bank transfer looks ordinary, and a person with a clean record is less likely to be questioned.

What was exposed

The release does not describe stolen personal data, so this is not a data-breach story. What the victims exposed was trust, emotional attachment and their savings. The only "personal information" in play was whatever people shared on dating profiles and in private messages, and that is worth thinking about. Public photos, a stated marital status and a mention of recent bereavement are all details a scammer can use to choose and shape a target.

How it ended

This case ended with convictions, sentences and a restitution order. That is a good outcome by the standards of romance fraud, where money often leaves the country quickly. A restitution order is not the same as repayment, though. The Justice Department release states the amount ordered, not the amount recovered, and victims of fraud rings often recover only a fraction of what they lost.

What you can check

1Search the profile photo

Run a reverse image search on any dating profile photo. Stolen pictures often appear under other names.

2Treat any money request as a stop sign

A request to send, receive or forward money for someone you have not met in person is the clearest warning sign, however the story is framed.

3Be careful with your own bank account

Never open an account, receive a transfer or forward funds for an online partner. The Texas case shows that the intermediary is treated as part of the offense.

The lesson in one line

A romance scam needs two things: a convincing story and a place to put the money. Refusing the second one breaks the scheme, and it protects you legally as well as financially.

Key facts at a glance

  • Charges: conspiracy to commit wire fraud and conspiracy to commit money laundering
  • Trial: seven days, ending in a jury conviction in December 2023
  • Sentence: 10 years on each count, concurrent, with an obstruction enhancement
  • Restitution: more than $2.2 million
  • Co-defendants: sentenced to 24 months (two people) and 37 months (two people)
  • Lead agency: IRS Criminal Investigation

Warning signs in this type of fraud

Romance fraud follows a recognizable arc. The first stage is rapid intimacy, with declarations of love or commitment arriving faster than the relationship has had time to develop. The second is a reason the person cannot be met, often a job abroad. The third is a financial problem that only you can solve. In this case the release describes a partner who could not reach his own bank accounts, which is exactly the kind of story that makes a transfer feel like a favor rather than a payment.

If you recognize the pattern in your own life, tell someone you trust before you act. Scammers rely on secrecy, and a second opinion often ends the scheme in a single conversation.

What the release does not say

The Justice Department release does not give a total number of victims, and it does not report how much of the restitution has been paid. It also does not say how the overseas contacts first reached the victims beyond naming the dating sites searched. We have kept this report to what the release states.

Frequently asked questions

Is receiving money for an online partner illegal?

It can be. In this case a jury convicted a woman of conspiracy to commit money laundering for receiving and passing on victims' money.

What did the court order for victims?

The Justice Department reports restitution of more than $2.2 million. A restitution order sets what the defendant owes, not what has actually been paid back.

Which dating sites were involved?

The release says the fraudsters searched sites such as Match.com and Zoosk. Scammers use any platform where people look for relationships, so the site is not the warning sign. The money request is.

How can I check if a profile is real?

Reverse-search the photos, ask for a live video call and be wary of anyone who always has a reason not to meet. Never send money to test them.
