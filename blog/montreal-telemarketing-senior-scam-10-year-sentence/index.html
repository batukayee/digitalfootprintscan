Montreal Telemarketer Gets 10 Years for Senior Scam

The caller said they were with your bank, your insurer or the government. They were not. They wanted your account details, and then the withdrawals started.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Canadian Press reported in August 2024 that a Pennsylvania judge sentenced a Montreal man to 10 years in prison, with 14 months already served deducted, and ordered US$7 million in restitution. He had pleaded guilty to wire fraud and money laundering in a telemarketing scheme that targeted tens of thousands of US seniors. Learn how scammers get your number.

Telemarketing fraud has a long history, and it has long relied on the same trick: get the person to hand over their own bank details. This Montreal-linked case reached tens of thousands of older Americans from call rooms in Canada.

What happened

Canadian Press reported that a Pennsylvania federal judge sentenced a Montreal man to 10 years in prison, with 14 months already served deducted, and ordered US$7 million in restitution. He pleaded guilty in January 2024 to three counts of wire fraud and four counts of money laundering, and was extradited from Canada in 2023.

The indictment, as reported, says he and his partners took US$13 million from victims since May 2011. The victims were tens of thousands of American seniors.

How it worked

Telemarketers claimed to be calling on behalf of victims' banks, insurers or the US government. They sold worthless products, including fraud protection, prescription savings cards and legal services. Once victims gave their banking information, the company debited their accounts.

According to the report, most of the telemarketers worked from boiler rooms in Montreal, and some in India. The structure was simple: a caller with a script, a payment form and an account number from a trusting victim.

What was exposed

The thing victims handed over was their bank account information. That is enough to authorize recurring debits, and a victim may not notice small charges for weeks. The report does not describe other data held by the callers.

The harm does not end with the money. A person who has given account details to a caller should assume those details are no longer private and ask the bank to monitor or replace the account.

How it ended

The case ended with a prison term and a restitution order. A sentence of 10 years, with time served deducted, is a serious outcome for a fraud case. The restitution of US$7 million is about half the US$13 million in the indictment. Whether victims will receive that money depends on what can be collected, and the report does not say how much has been paid.

Key facts at a glance

  • Sentence: 10 years, with 14 months already served deducted
  • Restitution: US$7 million ordered
  • Plea: guilty in January 2024 to three counts of wire fraud and four of money laundering
  • Extradition: from Canada in 2023
  • Indictment: US$13 million taken since May 2011
  • Victims: tens of thousands of US seniors

How to protect yourself and older relatives

1Never give account details to someone who called you

Whichever organization they name, hang up and call the number on your card or statement.

2Be wary of "protection" offers

A caller who sells fraud protection, prescription savings cards or legal services over the phone is a warning sign.

3Check statements for small recurring charges

Unfamiliar debits should be disputed with your bank at once.

What the report does not say

The Canadian Press report does not say how callers chose their targets or how much of the restitution has been paid. For a US case with another phone-based impersonation script, see the tech support scam ring sentenced in New York.

Why this case matters

This case sits at the intersection of two trends: scams that cross borders and scams that target older people. The callers were in Canada and India, the victims in the United States, and the court was in Pennsylvania. Extradition made it possible to prosecute the case, which is not always achievable.

For families, the lesson is that a polite, professional-sounding call with a convincing script is not evidence of legitimacy. People who run these operations are paid to sound trustworthy. A good habit for older relatives is a simple rule: never read out bank details on a call you did not place yourself, whatever the caller says. Families can also agree to review statements together every month.

They sold worthless products by phone, then debited victims' accounts once the victims gave their banking information.

Where were the telemarketers located?

Is restitution the same as getting money back?

No. A restitution order says what the offender must repay, but the amount collected depends on what can be recovered.
