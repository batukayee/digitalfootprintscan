Dating Site Fraud: Six Years for a $595,000 Scheme

He had a name, a job and a story built to fit her hopes. None of them were real. This Ontario case shows how a romance scam can be a long con that looks like a relationship.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Global News reported that an Ontario Superior Court judge sentenced a man to six years in September 2020 for defrauding a woman he met on eHarmony in July 2013. He posed as a financier and persuaded her to hand over $595,000 to invest. He was ordered to repay $558,456, and his lawyer said he would appeal. Romance fraud rarely begins with a request for money. It begins with attention, shared interests and a story that explains why the other person cannot meet in person yet or does not need to. The Ontario case below followed a recognizable pattern: a real-sounding career, a new relationship and then an investment.

What happened

Global News reported that the man met the woman on the dating site eHarmony in July 2013. According to the report, this was shortly after he had left prison following earlier frauds. He presented himself as a successful financier under an alias, and she gave him $595,000 to invest.

In September 2020, Ontario Superior Court Justice Beth Allen sentenced him to six years, with credit for 462 days of pre-trial custody. He had been convicted in May 2019.

How it worked

The scheme combined romance and finance. A dating profile built trust, the claimed career explained why he understood investments, and the relationship gave the request credibility. Investment advice from a partner feels different from a stranger's pitch.

The report adds that he spent the money on a new BMW and on gambling debts, and that he was romancing another woman at the same time. In other words, the financier identity and the investment were fiction. The story was a cover for taking the funds.

What was exposed

There was no database breach in this story, and the report does not describe any data exposure. What was exposed was a person's trust and savings. The only identity element described is an alias, a false name used to hide a criminal history.

That is the lesson for people who think a fraud needs a hack. A name that cannot be checked is itself a risk.

How it ended

The court ordered him to repay $558,456 within five years of his release and imposed a fine of the same amount, reduced by any repayment. He is also barred from controlling other people's property or finances. His lawyer said he maintains his innocence and would appeal.

An order to repay is not the same as repayment. We could not confirm the outcome of the appeal or whether any restitution was collected, so we do not state either.

How to spot the pattern

1Check who they are before money enters the picture

Search the name with the claimed employer, ask for a video call and run a reverse image search on profile photos.

2Treat any request to move money as a red flag

Whether it is an investment, a loan or a fee, a partner you have not met in person should not be asking.

3Slow the decision down

A scammer wants speed. Talk to a trusted friend or a bank advisor before moving a large sum.

What the report does not say

The article does not say how the woman was found, whether other victims were involved or what became of the appeal. For a case that traced a romance scam through its money mules, see the Texas money mule case.

Why this case matters

The timeline is the striking part. The relationship began in 2013, the conviction came in 2019 and the sentence in 2020. Romance fraud can take years to unravel, and victims often feel shame that keeps them from reporting, which gives fraudsters more time.

The case also shows that a person's past can be checked. The man had been released from prison after earlier frauds shortly before he met the victim. A basic search of court records and news coverage under a real name is not always possible when someone uses an alias, which is why independent verification of identity matters before any financial step.

Frequently asked questions

How much did the victim lose?

Global News reported that the woman gave the man $595,000 to invest. The court ordered him to repay $558,456 within five years of his release.

Was the man convicted?

Yes. He was convicted in May 2019 and sentenced on September 30, 2020 to six years, according to Global News. His lawyer said he would appeal.

Why do romance scams involve investments?

The relationship builds trust, and a claimed financial career makes an investment request seem natural. The money is then taken for the scammer's own use.

How can I check a person I meet online?

Reverse image search their photos, check the employer and name independently, ask for a live video call, and never send money to someone you have not met.
