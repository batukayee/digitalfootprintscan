LabHost: The Phishing Service With Over a Million Victims

Phishing used to need a skilled criminal. LabHost sold the fake pages, the support and the stolen data trail to anyone who paid a monthly fee.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

The Crown Prosecution Service says Zak Coyne was sentenced to eight and a half years at Manchester Crown Court on April 14, 2025 for running LabHost, which enabled thousands of criminals to defraud at least one million victims in 91 countries. UK losses exceeded £32 million. The April 2024 takedown involved partners in 18 countries. See what to do if your email was in a breach.

Phishing means tricking someone into entering their details on a fake page. LabHost made it into a subscription product. That changes the picture: a fraud that once took technical skill became something a customer could rent.

What happened

The CPS reported that Zak Coyne was sentenced to eight and a half years on April 14, 2025 at Manchester Crown Court. He had been arrested at Manchester Airport on April 14, 2024, exactly a year earlier, and pleaded guilty on September 18, 2024.

According to the CPS, LabHost enabled thousands of criminals worldwide to defraud at least one million victims in 91 countries. UK losses exceeded £32 million, and the estimated global loss was at least £100 million. The takedown in April 2024 involved partners in 18 countries.

How it worked

LabHost sold subscription access to phishing pages that imitated major banking, government and commercial websites, with technical support over encrypted messaging. A customer did not have to build a convincing fake login page; they chose one from the catalog.

Subscribers collected as much victim information as possible, to sell or use for fraud, including authorised push payment fraud, in which a victim is tricked into sending money themselves. Coyne received $230,000 in cryptocurrency.

What was exposed

The credentials and personal details of people who typed them into the fake pages were exposed. The CPS release does not say what kinds of data or what volumes were collected, and we do not guess. The common risk with any phishing kit is that what is entered into the fake page is immediately in the criminal's hands.

How it ended

For the criminal, the outcome was a long prison sentence. For the one million victims, the picture is less clear. The release does not describe any repayment, though the UK also introduced mandatory reimbursement rules for certain bank transfer scams in October 2024, which are covered in our article on the 88 percent reimbursement figures.

How the case unfolded

The arrest came at Manchester Airport on April 14, 2024. A guilty plea followed on September 18, 2024, and the sentence on April 14, 2025. Coyne received $230,000 in cryptocurrency from the operation, according to the CPS.

The investigation depended on international cooperation, which is typical for services that sell to customers in dozens of countries. A site run from one country can harm people in ninety others, and stopping it needs police in many places to act together.

How to avoid fake login pages

1Type your bank's address yourself

Or use its official app. Do not follow links in messages.

2Use a password manager

A manager usually will not autofill a login on a lookalike domain, which acts as a warning.

3Turn on multi-factor authentication

An entered password alone then does not open the account.

4Act fast if you typed details on a suspicious page

Contact your bank, change passwords and see what to do after an email breach.

What the release does not say

The release does not say how many fake sites LabHost hosted or how many people lost money in total. For an earlier case of fake bank calls offered as a service, see iSpoof.

Why this case matters

LabHost shows the industrial side of phishing. A criminal service with customer support and a product catalog produces more fraud than any single operator could. Taking it down in April 2024, with partners in 18 countries, removed a tool used by thousands.

It also shows why the old advice about spotting badly written fake pages no longer works well. Pages built from templates can look exactly like the real thing. The reliable check is not the look of the page but the route you took to reach it.
