How to Opt Out of People-Search Websites: A Complete Routine

Opting out of one people-search site takes ten minutes. Staying off all of them takes a system. This is the routine we recommend.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 26, 2026
TL;DR

Find every site that lists you, prepare a separate email and the exact listing URLs, then opt out of the biggest sites first. Confirm each removal with a fresh search, log the date, and recheck every three months. Each site needs its own request, and sites in the same corporate family may share one opt-out.

People-search websites sell reports that tie your name to your addresses, phone numbers, relatives and age. Almost all of them let you opt out for free. The hard part is not any single form. It is that there are dozens of sites, they copy from one another, and listings can return months later.

This guide is the routine: how to find which sites list you, what to have ready, the order to work in, and how to keep your listings from coming back. For step-by-step instructions on a specific site, use the individual guides linked below.

Step 1: Find out which sites list you

Search your full name with your city and state, then with a past city. Open the people-search results that appear on the first two pages and check whether they really are you. Common names produce plenty of strangers, so confirm at least two details, such as a relative's name and a past address. If a site shows several profiles for you, record each one; each profile usually needs its own request.

Not sure who a listing really belongs to, or which company runs the site? See how to identify which data broker a listing comes from.

Step 2: Prepare before you submit anything

1A separate email address

Most forms send a confirmation link. Using an alias keeps those messages in one place and avoids giving brokers your main inbox. See email aliases.

2The exact listing URL

Many forms ask you to paste the address of your profile page. Copy it while the listing is open and save it in your log.

3A removal log

Record the site, URL, date submitted, method (form, email or phone) and the date to recheck. You will need it when a listing returns.

4A rule for what you share

The FTC notes that opting out can require some information to verify your identity. Give only what the form needs. Be cautious with any site that asks for an ID upload when a simple form exists elsewhere.

Step 3: Opt out in this order

Start with the sites that rank highest for your name, because those are the ones people actually see. In the US that usually means:

  • Whitepages and Spokeo
  • TruePeopleSearch and FastPeopleSearch
  • BeenVerified, PeopleLooker and TruthFinder
  • Intelius, US Search, PeopleFinders and Instant Checkmate
  • Radaris, MyLife, Nuwber and USPhoneBook

Some of these brands belong to the same company. Opting out through the parent's central page can cover several sites at once, and the individual guides say when that is the case. Then move on to the smaller sites that appeared in your search.

Then the smaller and specialist sites

Once the big sites are done, search again. Smaller sites often show up only after the larger ones disappear from the first page of results. Common ones include CheckPeople, FamilyTreeNow, ClustrMaps, That's Them, AllPeople, America Phone Book, Spy Dialer, SearchPeopleFree and SmartBackgroundChecks. Behind the consumer sites are larger brokers such as LexisNexis, whose data feeds many other products.

Common problems and how to get past them

  • "No record found" on the opt-out form. Search with the exact spelling, middle initial and city shown on the listing. Some forms only match the profile URL, so paste it rather than typing your name.
  • The confirmation email never arrives. Check spam and promotions folders, then try a different address. Some sites block alias domains; a separate standard email account works as a fallback.
  • The site asks for ID or a phone call. Look for an alternative method on the site's privacy page first. If you must send a document, cover everything except your name and the details being removed.
  • The listing returns with a slightly different name. New records can create a new profile rather than restoring the old one. Treat it as a new listing and submit it again.
  • The form is broken or ignored. Email the site's privacy contact with the URL and the date of your first request. If you are in a state with a privacy law, say that you are making a deletion request under it.

Step 4: Confirm the removal actually worked

A confirmation email does not mean the listing is gone. After the stated processing time, open the saved URL in a private window and search the site again by name. If the profile still loads, resubmit, and use the site's contact address if the form keeps failing. Google may keep showing an old snippet for a while; once the page is gone, Google's Refresh outdated content tool can speed up the update. Our guide on how to verify a removal actually worked covers the details.

What about relatives who list you?

Even after your own listing is gone, your name can still appear as a "possible relative" or "associate" on a parent's, sibling's or partner's report. The FTC notes that your details may still show up in reports about relatives, neighbours or associates. The most effective fix is to work through opt-outs together as a household: one person keeps the log, everyone submits their own requests, and you recheck on the same schedule. If a family member does not want to opt out, ask whether they would at least remove the listing that shows your shared address.

Step 5: Recheck every three months

The FTC is direct about this: check the sites periodically and submit a new request if information reappears. Listings return because sites refresh from public records and buy data from one another. A quarterly reminder to rerun your step 1 searches is usually enough. If the same site keeps relisting you, see what to do when removed information keeps reappearing.

What opting out does not do

  • It does not delete the public record behind the listing, such as a deed or voter file.
  • It does not remove you from reports about relatives or neighbours, where your name may still appear as an associate.
  • It does not remove the listing from other sites, even ones that show identical data.
  • Some sites keep certain information, such as sex-offender status, which the FTC lists as an example of data that may not be removable.

How long the first round takes

Expect the first full pass to take a few hours spread over a week or two, mostly waiting for confirmation emails and processing times. Do the big sites in one sitting, the smaller ones in a second, and the verification pass a week later. After that, quarterly checks are usually much quicker, because you are only dealing with what changed. If you would rather not spend the time, a removal service does the same steps for you.

Using your legal rights alongside opt-outs

Opt-out forms are voluntary on the site's part. In some places you have a legal right as well. California residents can use the state's free DROP platform to send one deletion request to every registered data broker; from August 1, 2026, brokers must process those requests at least every 45 days. Our guide to the California Delete Act and DROP explains how. Other US state laws and the GDPR give similar deletion rights; see how to send a deletion request.

Frequently asked questions

How many people-search sites are there?

There are dozens of consumer people-search sites, plus hundreds of other data brokers. Most people only need to deal with the 15 to 25 sites that actually show their name in search results.

Do I need to opt out of each site separately?

Usually yes. The exception is when several brands share a parent company with a central opt-out page.

Is it safe to give a people-search site my email address?

Use an alias or a separate address. Only provide what the form requires, and avoid sending ID documents unless there is no alternative.

How long do opt-outs take?

Many sites remove a listing within a few days of confirmation. Others take a few weeks. Search engines can take longer to drop the old page.

Why should I recheck if I already opted out?

Sites rebuild profiles from public records and other brokers, so a new listing can appear later. The FTC recommends periodic checks.
