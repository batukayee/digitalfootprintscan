Bank Insider Fraud in Charlottetown: 16 Customers Hit

Customers trusted a branch employee with their accounts. Over three months he used that access to take their money, and it was discovered only after one customer noticed a missing statement.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

CBC reported that a former Toronto-Dominion Bank teller in Charlottetown was sentenced on September 18, 2018 to six months in jail, followed by six months of house arrest as part of two years of probation. He created two fake accounts and drew bank drafts payable to himself, taking $251,535.80 from 16 customers over three months. TD Bank reimbursed all customers. See how to prevent identity theft.

Most account-protection advice assumes the threat comes from outside: a hacker, a scammer, a stolen card. Insider fraud flips that. When a person with legitimate access abuses it, many controls built for strangers are not triggered.

What happened

CBC reported that a teller at the Toronto-Dominion Bank branch on Queen Street in Charlottetown pleaded guilty to fraud. He was sentenced in Charlottetown Provincial Court on September 18, 2018, to six months in jail, followed by six months of house arrest as part of two years of probation. The judge also ordered him to repay the bank. The Crown had sought 12 to 15 months, and the defence had proposed six months.

The total missing was $251,535.80. He defrauded 16 customers over three months. Twelve were elderly, and four accounts belonged to estates of recently deceased people.

How it worked

According to CBC, he created two fake bank accounts and drew bank drafts payable to himself. The first sign of trouble was $5,000 missing from one account. A forensic audit followed after an elderly customer complained about a missing monthly statement.

The detail that began the investigation is worth noting. A missing statement is the kind of small anomaly that people often ignore. Here, one customer's complaint led to a forensic review that found the rest.

What was exposed

The customers' money was taken from their accounts using access that the teller held because of his job. The report does not describe any other data theft. It does highlight the groups the fraud affected: older customers and the estates of people who had died, who may be less likely to review statements closely.

CBC reported that about $84,059.80 of the funds was used to buy a 2018 Audi, which was to be sold at auction. The judge commented that there was "clearly a personal gain factor."

How it ended

For the customers, the ending was good in the way that matters most: CBC reported that TD Bank reimbursed all of them. For the offender, there was a jail term, house arrest, probation and an order to repay the bank. The judge said low sentences can cause the public to lose confidence in the justice system, and the Crown called a quarter-million dollars large-scale fraud.

Key facts at a glance

  • Sentenced: September 18, 2018
  • Sentence: six months in jail, then six months of house arrest, within two years of probation
  • Amount taken: $251,535.80
  • Customers affected: 16, over three months
  • Vehicle bought with proceeds: a 2018 Audi, $84,059.80
  • Customer outcome: TD Bank reimbursed all customers

How to catch insider and account fraud early

1Open and read every statement

A missing statement, as in this case, can be the first clue. Contact the bank if one does not arrive.

2Use online alerts

Set up alerts for withdrawals, transfers and new payees on every account you hold.

3Check accounts of relatives you look after

If you manage an elderly relative's or an estate's finances, review the statements regularly.

4Report anything odd right away

Keep records and ask the bank to investigate in writing. Our guide on preventing identity theft covers further checks.

What the report does not say

The article does not say how the bank's own controls missed the fake accounts for three months, or whether the bank changed its procedures afterward. For a bank insider case from Australia, see the bank employee who opened accounts in customers' names.

Why this case matters

Insider cases are uncomfortable because they involve trust that we cannot avoid. Anyone with a bank account has to rely on staff. The protection is layered: audits, dual controls and customers who read their statements. In this case, the audit that found the fraud was triggered by a customer's report of one missing statement.

For families who manage money for older relatives or an estate, the practical step is a regular review. Fifteen minutes a month to read statements and check for unfamiliar payees can catch a problem long before it reaches the size of this one.
