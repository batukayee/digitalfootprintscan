London SIM Swap Case: How a Phone Number Became a Key

Four people lost control of their phone numbers in one week. The text messages that protected their accounts started arriving on someone else's phone.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Press reports quoting City of London Police say a man was sentenced to two and a half years at Inner London Crown Court in October 2026 for a SIM swap fraud from the first week of November 2021. Four victims' accounts were compromised, with losses of roughly £40,000, £17,000, £8,000 and £130,000. See what to do if your number leaks.

The case below is about a number most people treat as incidental: the phone number attached to their accounts. For the people in this case, losing control of it meant losing control of far more.

What happened

Press reports quoting City of London Police say a man was sentenced to two and a half years at Inner London Crown Court after pleading guilty in September 2026 to conspiracy to commit fraud by false representation and to transferring criminal property. The sentencing was reported on October 8, 2026.

Four victims' accounts were compromised in the first week of November 2021. The reports give losses of about £40,000, £17,000, £8,000 (on Coinbase) and £130,000. He was arrested in 2021 and again at Heathrow in October 2023. The money funded trips to Dubai and gold dental grills.

How it worked

Criminals moved victims' phone numbers to SIM cards they controlled and then intercepted the one-time codes sent by text. Those codes are what many banks and crypto platforms use to confirm a sign-in or a payment.

BT identified unauthorised activity on its systems, and data it provided helped police identify suspects, including the man who was sentenced. A detective described the offence as "a complex and calculated fraud."

What was exposed

What the thieves held was the victims' phone numbers, and with them the text messages that unlock accounts. The press reports do not say how the criminals obtained the victims' personal data to make the requests to the phone company, and we do not guess.

How it ended

Like many cyber-enabled frauds, this one took years. The offences were in 2021, the second arrest in 2023, and the sentence in 2026. The sentence of two and a half years is a conviction, but it does not state that the victims were repaid. We found no report of recovery.

A timeline of the case

The sequence runs across five years. The thefts happened in the first week of November 2021. The first arrest came in 2021, a second arrest at Heathrow followed in October 2023, a guilty plea came in September 2026 and the sentence was reported on October 8, 2026.

Because the sources are press reports rather than a court record, this timeline should be read as indicative. The detail worth taking from it is that the losses happened within days, while the justice process took years.

What the reports do not say

The reports do not say how the criminals got victims' details or whether any money was returned. For the same crime in another country, see the Massachusetts SIM swap case.

Why this case matters

Phone numbers were designed to reach people, not to prove who they are. Over time, banks, exchanges and email providers began to use them as proof, because sending a code by text was cheap and easy. A SIM swap exploits that shortcut.

The case also shows the value of telecom-side evidence. BT's records of unauthorised activity helped police identify suspects. For the account holder, the lesson is to remove the single point of failure: move to an authenticator app where possible and ask your provider for a PIN so that a request to move your number needs more than a few personal details.

Frequently asked questions

What is a SIM swap?

A SIM swap moves your phone number to a SIM card controlled by a criminal, so they receive your calls and text messages, including one-time codes.

What sentence was given?

Two and a half years at Inner London Crown Court, according to press reports quoting City of London Police.

How much did victims lose?

The reports give about £40,000, £17,000, £8,000 and £130,000 across four victims.

How can I avoid being SIM swapped?

Ask your provider for a port-out or SIM change PIN, use an authenticator app instead of SMS codes and act quickly if you lose signal.
