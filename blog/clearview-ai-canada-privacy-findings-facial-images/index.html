Clearview AI in Canada: Billions of Faces, No Consent

A photo you posted for friends can be copied by a company you have never heard of, turned into a biometric template and made searchable. Canadian regulators looked at exactly that.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

On February 2, 2021, the federal privacy commissioner and regulators in Quebec, British Columbia and Alberta published joint findings on Clearview AI. They found its database held over three billion facial images with biometric identifiers, including millions of images of people in Canada, collected without the required consent. Most people know that what they post can be seen by others. Fewer know that a company can copy their pictures by the billions, extract a unique template of each face and sell search access to others. That was the subject of a joint investigation by Canada's privacy regulators.

What happened

The investigation was run by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada together with Quebec's access to information commission and the privacy regulators of British Columbia and Alberta. It began in February 2020, and the findings were published on February 2, 2021.

According to the findings, Clearview's database held over three billion facial images with biometric identifiers, including millions of images of people in Canada, among them children. The regulators concluded that Clearview did not have the required consent and that its purposes were inappropriate.

How it worked

Images were collected from public websites and converted into biometric identifiers, so that a photo of a face could be compared against the whole database. Clearview offered the service to third parties. The findings say the RCMP was the only paying Canadian customer, and that Clearview had created 48 accounts for Canadian agencies and organizations, which ran thousands of searches.

The regulators found the purpose, mass identification and surveillance by a private company for commercial gain, to be inappropriate. The people whose images were collected were not asked.

What was exposed

The data was facial images and the biometric identifiers computed from them. A face is not like a password. It cannot be changed, and it is visible every time a person is photographed in public.

The findings also stress that the images were scraped from public websites. Being publicly viewable is not the same as consenting to collection for facial recognition, which is the principle the regulators applied.

How it ended

The matter was found well-founded. The regulators recommended that Clearview stop offering its service in Canada, stop collecting images of people in Canada and delete the images and facial arrays it already held. Clearview disagreed with the findings. It left the Canadian market in July 2020, having suspended access in March 2020 except for the RCMP. At the time of the findings, the company had not committed to follow the recommendations.

For the people whose pictures were collected, the practical result is uncertain. We have not reviewed whether any images were deleted.

Key facts at a glance

  • Findings date: February 2, 2021
  • Database size: over three billion facial images
  • Regulators: federal, Quebec, British Columbia and Alberta
  • Canadian accounts: 48 created for Canadian agencies and organizations
  • Only paying Canadian customer: the RCMP
  • Company response: disagreed and had not committed to the recommendations

What the findings do not say

The findings do not tell an individual whether their own photo was in the database or give a way to find out. They also do not describe any later outcome in court. For a case where regulators took action on location rather than face data, see the Tim Hortons app findings.

Why this case matters

The Clearview findings address a question that applies to everyone who has ever posted a picture: does public mean free for any use? The Canadian regulators said no. Collecting images from public websites to build a biometric database still required consent.

The case also shows the limits of regulation. When a foreign company disagrees with findings and leaves the market, regulators have fewer tools. For individuals, that makes personal habits more important than any one decision: think about which photos are public, and who can view them.
