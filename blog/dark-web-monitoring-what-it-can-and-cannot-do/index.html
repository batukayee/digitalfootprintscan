Dark Web Monitoring: What It Can and Cannot Do

Monitoring services sell peace of mind. Here is an honest look at what they can detect, what they cannot and how to decide whether you need one.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Dark web monitoring watches for your details in leaked data collections and alerts you. It cannot remove the data, stop criminals using it or see everything. Google ended its own dark web report in February 2026 because it gave information without clear next steps. Free tools such as breach alerts cover the basic job.

Dark web monitoring is one of the most heavily marketed privacy features. The pitch is simple: criminals trade stolen data in hidden corners of the internet, and a service will warn you if yours appears. The reality is more limited, and the market itself is changing.

This page is about evaluating monitoring services and the alerts they send. If you only want to check your details once, read dark web scan: how to check if your information is exposed.

What monitoring services actually do

We have not found an authoritative, vendor-neutral definition of what dark web monitoring covers, so this is our own summary of the common approach. Services collect data from breach disclosures, leaked databases and other sources, then compare it against the details you register, such as email addresses, phone numbers or ID numbers. A match triggers an alert.

That means monitoring detects exposure that has already happened. It does not prevent breaches and it does not retrieve the data.

What it can do

  • Tell you that an email address, password or identifier appeared in a collection the service holds.
  • Prompt you to change passwords or freeze credit sooner.
  • Save you the effort of rechecking breach lists manually.

What it cannot do

  • Remove your data. Once copied, it cannot be pulled back from criminals.
  • Cover everything. No service sees all collections. HIBP says absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and it holds only a subset of breached records.
  • Stop misuse. An alert arrives after exposure. Preventive tools, like a credit freeze and multi-factor authentication, act before misuse.
  • Fix the source. The compromised company or device still needs attention.

Google ended its dark web report

Google's support page states that scans for new dark web breaches stopped on 15 January 2026 and that the dark web report was no longer available from 16 February 2026, with related data deleted. Google's stated reason was that the report gave general information without clear next steps. It points users to Security Checkup, passkeys, Google Password Manager's Password Checkup and its Results about you tool instead.

That decision is a useful signal: a large provider concluded that alerts without clear actions were not helping. Read Google Results About You for the alternative it recommends.

Free alternatives

  • Have I Been Pwned. Search an email address and subscribe to Notify Me for future breaches.
  • Mozilla Monitor. It gets its breach data from HIBP and lets you check an email without an account. Alerts and the detailed report need a Mozilla account. Mozilla also shut down its paid Monitor Plus and its data broker scans, so do not expect removal services there.
  • Your own credit reports. In the United States, weekly free checks are available at AnnualCreditReport.com.

When paid monitoring might make sense

Paid services can bundle alerts, credit monitoring and support. If you want those conveniences, compare them on what you actually get: which identifiers are watched, how alerts are explained and what help you receive when something is found. Be cautious about claims of "full" dark web coverage or "guaranteed" protection. For how monitoring compares with other approaches, see are data removal services worth it.

Beware fake dark web alerts. The FTC warns that emails claiming your information is for sale on the dark web may be phishing. Do not click links or call numbers in them, and secure your email password first.

What to do when an alert arrives

1. Confirm the alert through the service's official site, not the message link. 2. Identify which data was exposed. 3. Change passwords and enable multi-factor authentication where passwords were involved. 4. Freeze credit if identifiers such as an SSN were involved. 5. Watch for related phishing.

Frequently asked questions

Is dark web monitoring worth paying for?

It depends on what else the service includes. The alert function alone is available free through breach lookups.

Can monitoring tell me who has my data?

Usually not. It reports that your data appeared in a collection, not who holds it or what they plan to do.

Does my data being on the dark web mean it is being used?

Not necessarily. Many collections are old or traded for little. Exposure raises risk but does not prove misuse.

Should I visit the dark web to check for myself?

No. It adds malware and legal risk for little benefit. Use established breach tools instead.

What replaced Google's dark web report?

Google points to Security Checkup, passkeys, Password Checkup and its Results about you tool.
