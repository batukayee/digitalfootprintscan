Data Broker and People-Search Statistics (2026)

How many data brokers are registered, how many people have asked to be deleted, how well opt-outs work and what regulators have done, using regulator releases and independent testing.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 12, 202628 statistics · 10 sourcesCite this page
TL;DR

More than 300,000 Californians had signed up for the state’s DROP deletion tool by June 2026, when CalPrivacy counted a record 581 registered data brokers. In the only independent test we found, Consumer Reports saw manual opt-outs remove 70% of profiles after a week, while paid removal services removed between 0% and 58.7%.

Key takeaways

  • California’s Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP) passed 300,000 sign-ups within about five months of its January 1, 2026 launch (CalPrivacy).
  • The registered broker list keeps growing: 459 in June 2025, more than 575 in February 2026 and 581 in June 2026 (IAPP; CalPrivacy). CalPrivacy’s DROP page now says “over 600”.
  • Enforcement has started. CalPrivacy’s announced actions include a $45,000 fine (January 2026) and a $116,490 fine (August 2026), both against data brokers.
  • Removal is slow and uneven. In a four-month Consumer Reports test, the best-scoring service with published results removed 68% of profiles and the lowest removed 4%, and no participant was fully cleared (Consumer Reports).
  • Public attitudes line up with the regulation: 73% of US adults say they have little or no control over data companies collect (Pew).

California DROP: sign-ups and brokers

California’s DROP lets residents send one deletion request to over 600 registered data brokers (CalPrivacy). CalPrivacy, the state privacy agency, runs the system, called DROP. Brokers must register each year and, from August 1, 2026, must check DROP at least every 45 days to process requests.

The August 2026 decision was described by law firm Orrick as the first CCPA decision against a data broker and the first under both the CCPA and the Delete Act. It alleged that the broker missed the January 31, 2025 registration deadline and required consumers to give a mailing address and part of a Social Security number to opt out. At the federal level, the FTC finalized an order on January 14, 2025 that bans Gravy Analytics and Venntel from selling sensitive location data except for limited national security and law enforcement purposes (FTC).

Do opt-outs work? The Consumer Reports test

Consumer Reports ran the most detailed independent test we found. It tracked 32 volunteers over four months across 13 people-search sites and 7 paid removal services priced from $19.99 to $249 a year. Its results are from 2024, so services may have changed, but the gap between manual and paid removal is the headline.

Methodology and limitations

Every figure on this page comes from the organization that published it, such as a regulator, a government agency or the original report. We read the source page or document directly, and we record the period each figure covers. When a number could not be confirmed against a primary source, we left it out. Rows marked “Our calculation” are simple arithmetic on published figures, and the inputs are named in the same row.

  • DROP participation counts are published by the agency and the press at different dates and with different labels, so they are shown as separate snapshots.
  • The Consumer Reports test is from 2024, covered 32 volunteers and does not represent every service or site. We have not seen a comparable independent test with newer results.
  • We did not find an authoritative count of people-search sites or of the share of adults listed on them, so we do not give one.
  • HIBP counts email addresses in a breach, which are not unique people.

Counts of reports, complaints and notifications measure what was reported or notified, not everything that happened. Currency symbols follow the source. Where an agency does not name its currency, we show the symbol the source uses (for example, C$ for Canada and A$ for Australia are our labels). Figures from different countries use different definitions and should not be added together. Spot an error? Tell us and include the source link; we correct verified errors and note the date at the top of the page.

Frequently asked questions

How many data brokers are there?

California’s registry is the best public count we found. It listed 459 brokers in June 2025, more than 575 in February 2026 and 581 in June 2026, and CalPrivacy now describes “over 600”. The registry only includes brokers that register, and it covers California (IAPP on DROP, Mar 2026; CalPrivacy: 300,000 sign up for DROP; CalPrivacy: DROP).

How many people have used California’s DROP tool?

CalPrivacy reported more than 300,000 Californians had signed up by June 2, 2026, and the IAPP reported more than 242,000 DROP requests as of a February 27, 2026 board meeting (CalPrivacy: 300,000 sign up for DROP; IAPP on DROP, Mar 2026). Our guide to how DROP works explains who can use it.

Do data removal services work?

In Consumer Reports’ 2024 test, results varied widely: after four months the highest published result was 68% of profiles removed and the lowest was 4%. Manual opt-outs removed 70% after one week (Consumer Reports, people-search removal study).

Have data brokers been fined?

Yes. CalPrivacy fined Datamasters $45,000 in January 2026 and an unnamed broker $116,490 in August 2026. The FTC banned Gravy Analytics and Venntel from selling sensitive location data in a final order in January 2025 (Covington: CalPrivacy $45,000 fine; Orrick: CalPrivacy data broker fine, Aug 2026; FTC Gravy Analytics order, Jan 2025).

Is it legal for people-search sites to list my information?

Legality depends on where you live and what the data is. Our explainer on whether people-search sites are legal covers it.
