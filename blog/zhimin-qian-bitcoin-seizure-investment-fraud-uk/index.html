UK's Biggest Bitcoin Seizure: A 128,000-Investor Scheme

The returns were promised, the early payouts were paid and the recruiters were rewarded. The seizure came years later and was described in one headline as record-breaking.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

Trade press quoting the Metropolitan Police and Al Jazeera report that Zhimin Qian was sentenced at Southwark Crown Court on November 11, 2025 to 11 years and 8 months after pleading guilty to two Proceeds of Crime Act offences. Some frauds are so large that the seizure of the proceeds is the headline. In this case, a record-breaking amount of cryptocurrency was held in a London home, and the open question is what happens to it next.

What happened

According to the coverage, Zhimin Qian was sentenced at Southwark Crown Court on November 11, 2025 to 11 years and 8 months. She pleaded guilty in September to two offences under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002, possessing and transferring criminal property. A second defendant, Seng Hok Ling, received 4 years and 11 months.

More than 128,000 investors, many of them elderly, were drawn into the scheme. Police seized more than 61,000 bitcoin from a house in Hampstead in 2018. The Metropolitan Police called the case one of the largest and most complex economic crime investigations it had ever undertaken, and the inquiry lasted seven years.

How it worked

Qian set up a company in China, Lantian Gerui, between 2014 and 2017. It claimed to develop technology and mine bitcoin. The scheme promised returns of 200 percent and paid early investors small daily payouts. Investors were paid to recruit others, and many took out high-interest loans to invest.

Small early payouts and paid recruitment are two classic hooks. The payouts make the opportunity seem real, and the recruitment turns every investor into a salesperson with a financial reason to persuade friends and family.

What was exposed

This was not a data breach. What investors lost was savings, handed over on the strength of trust, promised returns and referrals. The sources do not describe any personal data issue.

How it ended

The outcome is mixed. A long inquiry ended with a conviction and a sentence, and a large amount of cryptocurrency is in UK control. But the coverage says the seized bitcoin is expected to stay under UK control until further legal processes conclude, and victim compensation is uncertain. Al Jazeera adds that much of the defrauded money was recovered by police in China, but that a sizeable amount was siphoned off.

A timeline of the case

The sequence is long. The scheme ran from 2014 to 2017, police seized the bitcoin in 2018, the Metropolitan Police inquiry lasted seven years, and sentencing took place in November 2025. A guilty plea in September 2025 came shortly before the hearing.

That timeline is common in large financial crimes. Victims often wait years to learn whether any of the money will come back, and the decisions about the seized assets can depend on separate legal processes after the criminal case ends.

Key facts at a glance

  • Sentence: 11 years and 8 months (Qian) and 4 years and 11 months (Ling)
  • Sentencing date: November 11, 2025, Southwark Crown Court
  • Investors: more than 128,000
  • Bitcoin seized: more than 61,000 in 2018
  • Scheme period: 2014 to 2017
  • Promised return: 200 percent

How to spot the warning signs

1Be cautious when returns are promised

Very high guaranteed returns are a red flag, especially for new technology.

2Question paid recruitment

If you earn a reward for bringing in friends, that is a risk sign in itself.

3Check regulation

Ask whether a firm is authorized by the regulator for the service it offers.

4Do not borrow to invest

Why this case matters

The scale of the scheme, more than 128,000 investors, is a reminder that investment fraud is not limited to the gullible. It used the same human instincts that make normal investments appealing: a good return, a story about technology and friends who vouch for it.

The seizure also shows how cryptocurrency can be traced and held when a case reaches law enforcement. The unresolved question of who is repaid and how is typical of large frauds, where the legal process after a conviction can be long.
