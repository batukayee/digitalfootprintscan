Digital Privacy After a Divorce or Separation

Shared accounts and devices outlive the relationship. A calm, ordered review protects you without escalating anything.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

After a separation, the privacy risks are practical: shared passwords, joint accounts, family-plan location sharing, linked devices and cloud photo albums. Secure your email first, then change passwords and recovery options, review location and account sharing, and check what is public about your address. If you fear for your safety, address confidentiality programs exist in many places, and this guide is general information, not legal advice.

A relationship leaves digital traces: shared logins, joint subscriptions, synced photos and a trail of location data. When it ends, those links can be a source of stress or risk. The order in which you handle them matters.

Start with the accounts that protect everything else

Your primary email address is the key to the rest. Anyone who can read it can reset passwords for other accounts. Start there.

  • Change the password and sign out of all other sessions
  • Turn on two-step verification, preferably with an authenticator app or a passkey
  • Update the recovery phone number and recovery email
  • Check forwarding rules and connected apps for anything unfamiliar

The next layer is your phone carrier, Apple or Google account, and banking apps. Our guide to preventing identity theft lists the underlying protections.

Think before deleting shared evidence. If there are legal proceedings, deleting shared accounts or messages may cause problems. Ask your lawyer before removing anything that could matter.

Shared devices, plans and sharing features

Look for places where you may still be connected:

  • A family phone plan, where the plan owner can see account details
  • Shared cloud storage and photo albums
  • Location sharing in maps, family safety and messaging apps
  • Smart home devices, cameras and car apps with shared access
  • Streaming, shopping and delivery accounts that show addresses

Review each one, remove the other person's access when it is appropriate and safe to do so, and keep notes of what you changed. If you suspect monitoring software on a device, get advice from a trusted professional or support service before resetting it, since a reset can also destroy evidence.

Check your public exposure

A move or a name change creates new records. Your new address can reach people-search sites and other databases. Run a search of your name and old and new addresses, then follow how to check your data broker profiles after a life change.

Records such as voter registration also hold an address. NCSL reports that address confidentiality program participants are protected in nearly every state. California's Safe at Home program provides a substitute mailing address for eligible people, including victims of domestic violence and stalking. Read is voter registration public for the details.

A practical order of work

1Secure your email and phone number

Change passwords and recovery settings from a device the other person has not used.

2List every shared account

Write down banks, utilities, subscriptions, social accounts and cloud services that the two of you used.

3Remove access in a sensible order

Prioritize accounts that show location, money or private messages.

4Update your address carefully

Think about who needs your new address and who does not. Use a P.O. box or other mailing address where appropriate.

5Search yourself and clean up listings

Follow the address removal guide for listings that expose a new home.

6Re-check in a few weeks

New records take time to appear, so repeat the search later.

If you feel unsafe

If you have concerns about harassment, stalking or doxxing, read what is doxxing and how to protect yourself. The EFF recommends practical steps such as using address confidentiality programs, making social accounts private and checking where your handles are registered. For immediate danger, contact local emergency services.

Keep a simple record as you go

Write down what you change, when, and on which device. Take screenshots of anything suspicious, such as an unfamiliar login alert or a sharing setting you did not turn on. A short log helps if you later need to explain events to a lawyer, a service provider or the police, and it stops you repeating steps. Keep the log somewhere the other person cannot reach, such as a new account with a fresh password. This is general guidance, so follow your lawyer's advice about what to preserve.

Frequently asked questions

Should I change all my passwords after a breakup?

Start with your email, phone, banking and any account the other person knew or could reset. Then work through the rest.

Can my ex see my location?

Only if sharing is turned on in an app, device or plan you both use. Review each one.

Will changing my address hide it from people-search sites?

No. A new address can be picked up by brokers, so you should check and remove listings.

Should I delete shared photos and messages?

Ask a lawyer first if there are legal issues, since deleting could affect a case.

Is there a program that hides my address?

Address confidentiality programs exist in many states for qualifying people. California's Safe at Home is one example.
