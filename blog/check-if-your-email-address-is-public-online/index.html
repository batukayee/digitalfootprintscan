How to Check If Your Email Address Is Public Online

Your email address can be public in two very different ways: on pages anyone can read, and in breach data traded behind the scenes. Here is how to check both.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 26, 2026
TL;DR

Search your email address in quotes on Google and Bing, check it against known data breaches, look for it on people-search sites, and check places people forget, such as code commits, domain records and archived pages. Then fix or remove what you control, request removal from third-party pages, and secure any account tied to a breached address.

An email address being "public" can mean two things. It might be visible on a web page anyone can read, such as an old forum profile, a resume PDF or a people-search listing. Or it might sit in data from a breach, which is not indexed by search engines but circulates among criminals. Both matter, and they need different checks.

This guide walks through both, in order, and tells you what to do with each result.

Check 1: Search engines

Search your full address in quotes, for example "jane.doe@example.com", on Google and on Bing. The quotes force an exact match. Then try:

  • the part before the @ in quotes, such as "jane.doe", which often doubles as a username;
  • the address with filetype:pdf to find resumes, newsletters and documents;
  • the address with site: and a site name, such as a forum or social network you used.

Write down each page that shows the address and who controls it. Our self-search checklist covers more search patterns.

Check 2: Known data breaches

Search engines do not index breach data, so check it separately. Our free check on the home page uses breach data from Have I Been Pwned and shows which breaches included your address, when, and what types of data leaked. One caution from Have I Been Pwned's own FAQ: "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." A clean result means your address is not in the breaches it knows about, not that it has never leaked.

If you do find breaches, see what to do when your email is in a breach and what an exposed email address means.

Check 3: People-search sites

Many people-search sites let you search by email. Even if they do not, your email may appear in your name-based report. If it does, opting out of the listing removes it from that site; use our opt-out routine.

Check 4: Places people forget

1Code commits

If you have ever pushed code to a public repository, the commit author email may be visible. GitHub lets you use a GitHub-provided noreply address instead (GitHub Docs); turn this on for future commits.

2Domain registrations

If you registered a website, check whether your email shows in its domain records. See what WHOIS reveals.

3Archived pages

Old versions of web pages can survive on the Wayback Machine even after the original is gone. The Internet Archive accepts exclusion requests at info@archive.org, asking for the URLs and time period (Internet Archive Help).

4Documents and mailing lists

Search the address with filetype:pdf, filetype:doc and the names of clubs, schools or employers. Public mailing-list archives are another common source.

5Social and messaging apps

Check whether people can find your profile by email in social apps. See whether someone can find your social accounts with your email.

Check 5: Work and old addresses

Do not stop at your personal address. Work email addresses are often published on company sites, conference pages and press releases, and they appear in breach data whenever a supplier your employer uses is hacked. If a work address is exposed, tell your IT or security team rather than trying to remove it yourself. Old addresses from a previous job, university or internet provider matter too: you may no longer read them, but services may still use them for password resets. For each old address you find, either update the accounts that use it or make sure it is properly closed.

Finally, check the reverse direction. Some caller ID and contact apps build profiles from uploaded address books, which can tie your email and phone number together; see how to unlist your number from Truecaller.

What to do with each result

A page you control

Edit or delete the address, or hide it in the profile settings. Then ask search engines to refresh the result.

A page someone else controls

Contact the site owner and ask for removal. If the page also exposes other contact details, Google may remove it from results under its personal information policy.

Once a page has changed, Google's Refresh outdated content tool updates the old snippet. For pages that still show your email next to your name, Google's Results about you tool and its personal information removal policy cover contact details including email addresses. Removal from Google does not delete the page itself.

Is a public email address a problem?

Not always. A work address on a company site or a contact address on your own business page is meant to be public. The risk comes when your personal address, especially the one used to log in to banking or email, is easy to find. It makes phishing more convincing, helps attackers guess which services you use and is the starting point for credential stuffing after a breach.

A practical fix is to separate roles: one private address for important accounts, and aliases or plus addresses for shopping, newsletters and sign-ups. If an old address is everywhere and no longer used, see what to do about an old email that is still public.

What people can do with a public email address

  • Targeted phishing. An address found next to your name and employer lets a scammer write a convincing message that appears to come from your bank, your boss or a delivery company.
  • Account discovery. Some services reveal whether an email is registered through sign-up or password-reset pages, letting someone map which services you use.
  • Linking identities. The same address on a forum, a resume and a people-search listing connects those profiles to one person.
  • Password attacks. If the address appears in breach data, attackers try leaked passwords on other sites. A unique password for every account defeats this.

Stop your email becoming public again

On your own website or business page, use a contact form instead of a clickable email link, or a role address such as hello@ that you can retire. Turn off email visibility in social and forum profiles, use a noreply address for code commits, and give each newsletter or shop its own alias so you can see who leaked or sold it. If one alias starts receiving spam, switch it off. Our guide to stopping spam covers the inbox side.

How often to check

Run the breach check and the quoted search every few months, and right after news of a major breach. You can set a Google Alert for your email address in quotes so new public mentions are emailed to you. For the full picture beyond email, see our email exposure check guide.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find out where my email address is used?

Search it in quotes, check it against breach data, and search your inbox for sign-up and welcome emails to list the services you used it for.

Can I remove my email address from Google?

Google can remove results that expose contact details under its policy, but the page itself stays online unless the site owner removes it.

Does a public email address mean I was hacked?

No. It means the address is visible. A breach is different and is checked separately.

Is it safe to type my email into a breach checker?

Use one that does not store the addresses you check. Our check does not store your email, and Have I Been Pwned says its searches are not logged.

Should I change my email address if it is public?

Not necessarily. Make sure the account has a strong, unique password and two-step verification. Changing makes sense if the address is in many breaches or attracts heavy spam.

What is the difference between a public email and a breached email?

A public email is visible on web pages anyone can open. A breached email was leaked from a company's systems and circulates in data sets that search engines do not show. You need both checks to see the full picture.
