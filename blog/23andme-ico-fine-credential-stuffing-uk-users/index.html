23andMe Breach: Reused Passwords Exposed 155,592 UK Users

Nobody hacked a DNA database. Attackers logged in with passwords people had already used somewhere else, and the site did not require a second check.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

On June 17, 2025, the UK Information Commissioner's Office fined 23andMe £2.31 million after credential stuffing attacks that began in April 2023 gave attackers access to the personal information of 155,592 UK residents. The ICO found no mandatory multi-factor authentication and weak password protocols. See how credential stuffing works.

Genetic ancestry services hold information that is deeply personal and permanent. The 23andMe case is about how that kind of information was reached: not by a technical break-in, but by logins that attackers already had.

What happened

The ICO fined 23andMe £2.31 million, announced on June 17, 2025. It found that 155,592 UK residents had personal information accessed without authorization. The credential stuffing attacks began in April 2023. In October 2023, an employee found the stolen data advertised for sale on Reddit, and 23andMe confirmed a breach.

The ICO's investigation was carried out jointly with the Privacy Commissioner of Canada. The regulator received 12 complaints.

How it worked

Credential stuffing means trying logins that were leaked in earlier, unrelated breaches. If a person reused the same email and password on 23andMe, the attacker could simply sign in. The ICO found that 23andMe lacked mandatory multi-factor authentication, secure password protocols and unpredictable usernames.

It also found no additional verification step for downloading raw genetic data, and inadequate threat monitoring and incident response. Taken together, the ICO's view was that the company did not put enough protection around some of the most sensitive data a service can hold.

What was exposed

The ICO says the information included names, birth years, self-reported location, profile images, race, ethnicity, family trees and health reports. The exact data varied by account.

Information Commissioner John Edwards said the information "cannot be changed or reissued like a password or credit card number." A leaked password can be replaced, but a family tree or health history cannot. That permanence is why the regulator treated the lack of basic protections so seriously.

How it ended

The ICO fine is the outcome in the release. As with most fines, it is paid to the government and not to the people affected. The release does not describe compensation for individuals.

The case is also an example of how a breach can be detected late. The attacks started in April 2023, and 23andMe confirmed the breach only after an employee noticed the data being sold in October 2023.

A timeline of the case

The sequence is short but telling. Credential stuffing began in April 2023. For about six months nobody inside the company raised the alarm. In October 2023, an employee noticed the stolen data on Reddit, and 23andMe confirmed the breach. The ICO fine followed in June 2025, nearly two years later.

The gap between the start of the attacks and their discovery is the key detail. It matches the ICO's finding of inadequate threat monitoring and incident response.

Key facts at a glance

  • Fine: £2.31 million, announced June 17, 2025
  • UK residents affected: 155,592
  • Attack began: April 2023
  • Discovered: October 2023, after an employee found the data for sale
  • Cause: credential stuffing using reused passwords
  • Missing controls: mandatory multi-factor authentication, secure password protocols

Why this case matters

It shows that user behavior and company design are linked. A person who reuses a password has taken a risk, but the ICO placed the responsibility for stronger defaults on the service. Mandatory multi-factor authentication would have stopped logins that had only a leaked password.

For customers, the lesson is dual. Use a unique password everywhere, and treat genetic and health services as high-value accounts that deserve extra protection. If you ever shared a family tree or ancestry results, remember that relatives can be affected by what you chose to make visible, even if they never created an account themselves.
