SIM Swap and Phone Number Fraud Statistics (2026)

How often criminals take over phone numbers, what it costs and how regulators have responded, using FBI complaint data, UK fraud-database figures and Australian and US regulator actions.

By Siddharth ShankarUpdated October 12, 202627 statistics · 10 sourcesCite this page
TL;DR

The FBI logged 971 SIM swap complaints with $17.4 million in reported losses in 2025, down from 1,075 complaints and $48.8 million in 2023. In the UK, Cifas saw about 3,000 unauthorised SIM swaps in 2024, up 1,055%, and unauthorised SIM swaps rose another 38% in 2025. Australia’s regulator penalized Telstra A$1,551,000 for skipped identity checks.

Key takeaways

  • US SIM swap complaints to the FBI have been flat to falling: 1,075 in 2023, 982 in 2024 and 971 in 2025, and reported losses fell from $48.8 million to $17.4 million (IC3).
  • The average reported loss per complaint fell from about $45,394 in 2023 to about $17,885 in 2025 (our calculation). SIM swap remains a very small share of FBI complaints, about 0.1% in 2025.
  • The UK trend runs the other way. Cifas recorded nearly 3,000 unauthorised SIM swaps in 2024 (+1,055%) and a further 38% rise in 2025.
  • Phone accounts are an entry point to other accounts: 48% of UK account takeover cases in 2024 involved mobile phone accounts (Cifas).
  • Regulators are acting on the carriers. The FCC adopted SIM-swap and port-out rules in November 2023, and ACMA penalized Telstra A$1,551,000 in 2024.

FBI complaints about SIM swapping

A SIM swap moves a victim’s phone number to a SIM card controlled by a criminal, who can then receive the one-time codes sent by text. The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center reports SIM swapping as its own category. It captures only what victims report to the FBI.

Regulator action on carriers

In the US, the Federal Communications Commission adopted rules in November 2023 (FCC 23-95) requiring wireless carriers to use secure methods of authenticating customers before a SIM change or port-out, and to notify customers of such requests (FCC). In Australia, the Communications and Media Authority’s rules on high-risk customer transactions took effect on June 30, 2022.

Methodology and limitations

Every figure on this page comes from the organization that published it, such as a regulator, a government agency or the original report. We read the source page or document directly, and we record the period each figure covers. When a number could not be confirmed against a primary source, we left it out. Rows marked “Our calculation” are simple arithmetic on published figures, and the inputs are named in the same row.

  • SIM swap counts are small, so year-to-year changes can be noisy, and categories differ: the FBI counts complaints, Cifas counts filings by member organizations.
  • The FBI and Cifas figures measure different things and different countries and cannot be added.
  • We did not find a primary-source SIM swap figure for Canada or Australia for 2025, so none is shown. The ACMA $28,000 average is from 2022, covers scams targeting telco customer interactions rather than SIM swaps alone, and the ACMA does not give a dataset for it.
  • Currency symbols for Australian figures follow the ACMA releases, which use $. We label them A$.

Counts of reports, complaints and notifications measure what was reported or notified, not everything that happened. Currency symbols follow the source. Where an agency does not name its currency, we show the symbol the source uses (for example, C$ for Canada and A$ for Australia are our labels). Figures from different countries use different definitions and should not be added together. Spot an error? Tell us and include the source link; we correct verified errors and note the date at the top of the page.

Frequently asked questions

How common is SIM swap fraud?

In the US it is rare in complaint data: 971 FBI complaints in 2025 out of 1,008,597 total, about 0.1%. In the UK, Cifas recorded nearly 3,000 unauthorised SIM swaps in 2024 (FBI IC3, 2025 report; Cifas SIM swap release, May 2025).

How much do victims lose to SIM swapping?

FBI complaints averaged about $17,885 in 2025 and about $45,394 in 2023 (our calculation). ACMA has said scams that target customers’ dealings with telcos cost individual victims in Australia about A$28,000 on average (a broader category than SIM swapping) (FBI IC3, 2025 report; FBI IC3, 2023 report; ACMA: SIM-swap rules, Apr 2022).

Is SIM swap fraud increasing?

It depends on the country and the data. FBI complaints and losses fell between 2023 and 2025. Cifas reported a 1,055% rise in unauthorised SIM swaps in 2024 and a further 38% rise in 2025 (FBI IC3, 2025 report; Cifas SIM swap release, May 2025; Cifas Fraudscape 2026).

Have phone companies been penalized for SIM swap failures?

Yes. ACMA announced a A$1,551,000 penalty against Telstra in July 2024 for skipping identity checks on 168,000 high-risk interactions. The FCC adopted SIM-swap rules for US carriers in November 2023 (ACMA: Telstra penalty; FCC 23-95 order).

How can I protect my number?

Ask your carrier about a port-out PIN or number lock, avoid text-message codes where an authenticator app or passkey is available, and see what is public about your number. Our guide to checking if your phone number is public is a start.
