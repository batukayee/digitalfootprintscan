WickedGen: The Site That Sold Stolen Streaming Logins

A cheap streaming account sounds like a bargain. For the people whose logins were sold, it was theft, and the same stolen logins feed bigger attacks.

By Digital Footprint ScanUpdated October 11, 2026
TL;DR

The AFP says a 23-year-old Sydney man who ran WickedGen.com and related sites was sentenced in April 2021 to 2 years and 2 months, served as an intensive corrections order, and that $1.66 million was forfeited to the Commonwealth. The services had at least 152,863 registered users. He received more than $680,000 via PayPal. See how credential stuffing works.

Stolen logins are the raw material for many online crimes. Some are used to drain accounts. Others are packaged and resold in bulk. This Australian case concerned the second kind.

What happened

The AFP says a Sydney man conspired with a man in the United States to steal streaming-service login credentials and resell them cheaply. He ran WickedGen.com for about two years and also created or ran HyperGen, Autoflix and AccountBot.

He pleaded guilty in October 2020 and was sentenced in April 2021 to 2 years and 2 months, served as an intensive corrections order. The release was published on October 29, 2021, and the forfeiture order was made the week before.

How it worked

Across four services the sites had at least 152,863 registered users and provided at least 85,925 subscriptions. The FBI indictment alleged the conspiracy to create and run AccountBot ran from February 2018 to March 2019.

The man received more than AUD $680,000 via PayPal and converted some of it to cryptocurrency. The accounts that were sold belonged to unknowing victims in Australia and overseas.

What was exposed

Login credentials were harvested and sold. The release does not say how they were stolen. The same stolen credentials are also what credential stuffing attacks depend on, a link the release does not draw but that is worth knowing.

An owner of a streaming account may lose little except access and a charge. But if the same password protects an email or bank account, the exposure is much larger.

How it ended

The ending is relatively good. $1.66 million was forfeited to the Commonwealth, including over $1.2 million in cryptocurrency. The man received a sentence of 2 years and 2 months as an intensive corrections order. The release does not describe any payments to account holders.

A timeline of the case

The FBI indictment alleged the AccountBot conspiracy ran from February 2018 to March 2019. The Sydney man pleaded guilty in October 2020, was sentenced in April 2021 and, around the week before October 29, 2021, a forfeiture order was made. The AFP release followed.

The case therefore ran for more than three years from the alleged conduct to the order that took the money. Asset forfeiture happens after conviction and can take time, but in this case it recovered a large sum.

Key facts at a glance

  • Sentence: 2 years and 2 months, served as an intensive corrections order (April 2021)
  • Forfeited: $1.66 million, including over $1.2 million in cryptocurrency
  • Registered users: at least 152,863 across four services
  • Subscriptions provided: at least 85,925
  • Income: more than $680,000 via PayPal
  • AFP release: October 29, 2021

Why this case matters

It shows an economy around stolen credentials: some people steal, others resell, and customers who buy cheap accounts help fund it. A buyer may think only of saving money, but the account came from someone who did not agree to sell it.

It is also a reminder that your login can be sold without any dramatic breach of a large company. Individual accounts can be stolen one by one.

The cooperation behind the case

The AFP worked with the FBI because the conspiracy involved a man in the United States. That cross-border link explains how a Sydney resident came to be charged for a service whose customers were around the world.

For individuals, the point is that stolen credentials are an international commodity. A login stolen in one place can be sold in another within hours, so a quick password change is more effective than waiting to learn how the theft happened.
